## Slide 3　Review: boundaries and dates

Tree-ring boundary. Identify an annual increment from anatomical changes. False and locally absent rings. Counting one radius can introduce extra or missing years. Question for today. What can comparison with other trees establish?. A mistaken boundary can affect both width measurements and the calendar assignments of subsequent increments.

## Slide 4　Counting and crossdating

Approach. What it establishes. Ring counting. Counts visible increments; missed or extra boundaries can shift the result. Crossdating. Tests ring dates by matching shared patterns among series. Calendar anchoring. Connects the sequence to a securely dated reference. Counting gives the number of visible increments. Crossdating tests their individual years using shared variation in other series.

## Slide 5　Factors that limit growth

Water availability can limit growth at dry sites. Growing-season temperature can limit growth at cold sites. The relevant factor varies with species, site and period. Limiting factors differ among sites and species. Shared environmental responses provide one basis for matching growth patterns.

## Slide 6　Choosing sites and trees for the question

Climate questions. Select sites where trees respond to the climate variable of interest. Forest-history questions. Look for trees and spatial patterns recording competition or disturbance. Conditions for comparison. The same species or region does not guarantee matching growth. Choose trees for the required period and environmental information, not simply because they are old.

## Slide 7　Several influences contribute to ring width

Age and stem size. Shared environmental variation. Competition, disturbance and individual effects. Ring-width variation. Several influences contribute to width. Finding a shared pattern and identifying its cause are distinct tasks.

## Slide 8　Uniformitarianism and testing growth responses

Uniformitarianism. Use present processes to help interpret past records. Responses may change. Age, site and climate changes can alter growth responses. Reconstruction needs further tests. Verify dating and environmental interpretation separately. Applying a modern relationship to the past requires checking its conditions. It is not an assumption that every response remains unchanged.

## Slide 9　Crossdate a sequence, not one narrow ring

These wood strips have different starting and ending positions. Compare the narrow ring, nearby wider increments, and the pair of adjacent narrow rings near the green bands. The evidence is a sequence of several features, not one narrow ring. This idealized illustration duplicates a width pattern to make the alignment clear. Real trees differ and their measurements do not match perfectly. Dating therefore combines independent specimens, sufficient overlap, boundary observations, and statistical checks.

## Slide 10　Replication across radii, trees and sites

Multiple radii of one tree. Different trees. Different sites. Four samples need not represent four trees. Several radii help inspect local features, while additional trees provide different replication. Keep those information sources distinct.

## Slide 11　Why use a log10 axis for ring-width comparisons?

Both panels show the same widths. The green series is four times the orange series at every point. On a linear axis, its fluctuations look larger. On a log10 axis, equal ratios span equal vertical distances. A change from zero point five to one millimeter and a change from two to four millimeters are both doublings, so both span the same vertical distance. This makes relative changes in narrow rings easier to read and helps compare patterns between series with different width levels. Tick labels retain the original widths. The original measurements are preserved while the display scale changes. Zero has no finite position on a conventional logarithmic axis, so missing measurements must remain missing.

## Slide 12　The basis of crossdating

Compare the sequence of variation in different trees. Absolute widths can differ while shared features remain. Compare rises and falls around narrow rings as well as the narrow rings themselves.

## Slide 13　From one marker year to a sequence

A single narrow-ring match is insufficient. Check surrounding variation, spacing and the whole overlap. A narrow ring can recur many times. A matching sequence of several features provides stronger evidence for choosing among alignments.

The first part may match while the later part diverges. Investigate missed boundaries, locally absent rings or false rings. Do not insert or delete values merely to improve a match. ### Slide 22　Different radii and different trees provide different checks

Another radius helps test whether a growth layer continues elsewhere in the same tree. Another tree checks replication of the annual pattern independently. Original images help revisit boundaries and measurement paths. These checks address different questions. If alignment remains uncertain, retain that uncertainty rather than inserting or deleting values merely to improve agreement.

### Slide 23　Combine visual, statistical and independent checks

First seek candidate alignments using several features and their spacing. Then examine agreement across the interval, overlap length and statistics. A strong statistic is useful, but the highest value alone does not establish a date. Multiple tested positions, autocorrelation and short overlaps affect interpretation. Check whether other radii or trees support the calendar assignment, then return to images and measurements. Do not force disagreements into a match; retain an unresolved result when appropriate.

> https://www.ltrr.arizona.edu/skeletonplot/introcrossdate.htm

### Slide 24　A short overlap contains fewer pattern checks

A short overlap may show a similar peak or trough by chance. Longer overlap permits checking intervening patterns and additional marker years, but does not guarantee a correct match. Weak variability and autocorrelation also matter. Three and twenty years are illustrative, not pass/fail thresholds. Practising with thirty rings does not establish thirty rings as sufficient for real-world dating.

> https://www.ltrr.arizona.edu/skeletonplot/introcrossdate.htm

### Slide 25　Where to return when a match fails

Do not immediately insert or delete rings to improve a match. Return to images to inspect boundaries, possible discontinuities, paths and units, then compare another radius and other trees. Not every disagreement is a reading error: differing site conditions, species or climate responses may weaken commonality. Make revisions only with observational reasons, preserve original values and document the change. If independent evidence is insufficient, retain an unresolved date.

## Slide 26　Records supporting a dating decision

Sample ID, sampling position and observed boundaries. Reference and version, candidate dates and overlap. Matching and mismatching features, with rechecks. Handling of ring anomalies, analyst and date. Retain candidates, mismatches and reinspection locations so another reader can evaluate the dating decision later.

## Slide 28　Summary

Shared variation supports matching. Growth also contains individual and disturbance effects. Test ring dates with multiple features. Observed ring counts can differ from the calendar span. Replication and independent checks matter. Investigate mismatches as well as apparent agreement. Dating combines visual inspection, statistical support, replication and calendar anchoring, with none of those steps standing alone.
