## 30　Mount St. Helens: eruption effects on the Pacific margin

Locate Japan and western North America, then Mount St. Helens in Washington State. The star marks the volcano, not the precise photograph or sampling location. Earlier lecture photographs show a buried trunk and cross-section sampling. Compare wood evidence with deposits, topography, mortality and establishment. ## 31　Tunguska: a Siberian event and one surviving tree

Locate Siberia relative to Japan. The regional star marks the approximate Tunguska blast-centre area, not the sampled tree. The event occurred on 30 June 1908; atmospheric airburst is the basic modern explanation. Yonenobu and Takenaka (1998) studied one surviving spruce about 7.3 km north-northeast of the centre. Their Figure 2 shows the 1908–1909 wood. Traumatic resin ducts were observed near the 1908 earlywood-to-latewood transition; attributing them to blast or heat is interpretation. One tree does not identify the impactor.

- [Source 1](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033822200018245)
- [Source 2](https://www.nasa.gov/history/115-years-ago-the-tunguska-asteroid-impact-event/)
- [Source 3](https://tunguska.tsc.ru/files/3112/MainPart/earthmoonplanets.pdf)

## 35　Wrightwood: earthquake history in California

Locate the western USA and then Wrightwood in southern California. The star locates the town approximately, not an exact epicentre or fault trace. This case compares the December 1812 historical earthquake with sustained growth reduction from 1813 in trees near the San Andreas Fault. Drought can also produce narrow rings, so compare more distant trees and independent geological evidence. Location alone does not establish cause.

- [Source 1](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.241.4862.196)
- [Source 2](https://scedc.caltech.edu/earthquake/wrightwood1812.html)
