## Slide 1　Reading Environment and History through Tree Rings

Dendroecology. This explanation accompanies a ninety-minute dendroecology lesson. Pause to complete the calculation and comparison exercises.

## Slide 2　Learning objectives

Explain the questions addressed by dendroecology. Relate growth changes, scars and ages to forest history. Distinguish observed changes from hypotheses about causes. Consider not only how growth changes but also which comparisons can help evaluate its possible causes.

## Slide 3　Can insect damage leave a ring record?

Look for the white woolly masses on the twig beside the finger. These are hemlock woolly adelgid egg sacs. Insects damage trees in different ways; this is not a defoliating caterpillar. To connect signs on the twig with ring widths or wood anatomy in the stem, we also need infestation dates and suitable comparison trees.

## Slide 4　Three kinds of evidence for forest history

Growth: changes in ring width and their duration. Injuries: dated scars and subsequent wound closure. Populations: regeneration, establishment and mortality. Growth, injuries and population ages provide different information. Combining them helps evaluate the limitations of any single record.

## Slide 5　Canopy gaps and changing competition

A canopy gap changes light and competitive conditions. Responses depend on species, position and prior growth. Reconstruct stand dynamics from multiple individual histories. Responses to extra light differ among species and prior growth states. Consider the surrounding conditions before and after a gap opens.

## Slide 6　Canopy gaps and growth release

Follow the same surviving tree from left to right. Initially, taller neighbours shade its crown. When one neighbour falls, a canopy gap changes light and competition. The right scene illustrates a possible increase in growth over subsequent years. Wider outer rings follow the earlier narrow rings in the schematic section below. The tree does not become thicker at the instant its neighbour falls, and the response is not universal. Species, site, and the tree’s previous condition matter. In the curves that follow, distinguish an isolated wide ring from a sustained increase.

## Slide 7　Growth suppression and release

Suppression: a period of relatively slow growth. Release: a sustained increase in growth to investigate. One wide ring alone does not establish canopy opening. A sustained increase can be informative, while a single wide ring does not by itself establish release from competition.

## Slide 8　Different causes can produce similar changes

Consider competition, climate, water, insects and management. Distinguish immediate changes from delayed responses. Test a hypothesis with independent observations or records. Compare climate, thinning, recovery after insect damage and other explanations capable of producing a similar change.

## Slide 9　Decide what to compare before sampling

The photographs show increment coring and core extraction. A core alone cannot tell us the tree’s site or canopy position. Record species, stem diameter, site conditions, and damage under the specimen identifier. Plan comparison stands or non-host species as appropriate, and consider the bias introduced by sampling only obvious damage or surviving trees.

## Slide 12　Exercise A: does 100% mean the same thing?

## Slide 13　Exercise A: percentages and absolute changes

A and D both increase 100%, but by 1.0 and 0.1 mm. B increases 50%; C is unchanged. Compare the tree groups. The calculation alone does not establish a cause or event date. Equal percentage increases can represent very different added widths. Compare the starting level and absolute increment as well.

## Slide 14　Release criteria depend on the trees

Small prior growth can produce large percentage changes. Choose criteria appropriate to species and growth conditions. Do not turn 100% into a universal disturbance threshold. Evaluate prior growth and species differences rather than treating one numerical threshold as applicable to every tree.

### Slide 15　Equal ring widths can add different areas

The two schematics have different previous stem radii but the same added width. The larger stem has a longer circumference and therefore gains more cross-sectional area. For circular concentric growth, subtract the inner-circle area from the outer-circle area using previous radius r and width w. Ring width and basal area increment are different quantities. Real stems may be eccentric or irregular, so examine the radius and shape assumptions. Basal area increment alone does not directly determine whole-stem volume or biomass.

## Slide 18　Reading fire history from scars

The left photograph shows an external pine scar; the right shows repeated fire scars in a different tree. Look for growth around the wound margins and repeated injury positions. These are not before-and-after photographs of one specimen. Crossdate the injury, then assess its origin using wound characteristics and field evidence. ## Slide 19　New growth encloses an old wound

The four sections illustrate how subsequent growth can enclose a stem wound. Living margins on both sides produce new growth, approach each other, and eventually cover the damaged area. The old injured tissue is not restored to its original state: an internal scar remains. Species, injury depth, and decay affect the course of closure. In the specimen photographs, look for growth layers curving around the wound margins. The ring counts in this schematic cannot be used to measure how many years closure took.

## Slide 32　Tunguska: reading the forest response from injury and growth

The figure shows a ring-width index, not raw ring width. A cubic polynomial removed the general growth trend, so the original linear index axis is retained. The paper reports very low growth in nineteen oh nine through nineteen twelve and a sharp increase in nineteen thirteen. Injury, several years of suppression, and subsequent recovery are records with different timings. The authors interpreted them in terms of damage and recovery of photosynthetic organs, reduced competition, and changes in light and nutrients. These environmental factors were not individually manipulated in an experiment. Multiple radii from one tree also do not provide independent replication across trees.

### Slide 33　Hypothesized pathway: D–T fusion to ¹⁴C production

D–T fusion produces helium-4 and a neutron, releasing about 17.6 MeV; the neutron carries roughly 14.1 MeV. Nitrogen-14 can then capture a neutron and emit a proton to produce carbon-14. Check mass numbers and charge on both sides. Correct equations do not demonstrate fusion in cometary ice at Tunguska. Fuel, reaction conditions, neutron transport and moderation, and reaction probabilities require separate examination. Ordinary atmospheric radiocarbon production also involves cosmic-ray-generated neutrons. Separate the proposed additional neutron source from the established nuclear reaction.

### Slide 34　How does ¹⁴C enter a tree ring?

Produced carbon-14 enters atmospheric carbon dioxide through oxidation and other chemistry, then mixes in the atmosphere. The first arrows summarize a chemical pathway, not a nuclear or fully balanced chemical equation. Plants assimilate radiocarbon-bearing carbon dioxide through photosynthesis; isotope labels are omitted from the photosynthesis equation. Transport, metabolism and sometimes storage precede incorporation into wood cellulose. Production therefore does not imply immediate incorporation into one ring. Assess atmospheric mixing, assimilation season, stored carbon, sampling and precision. Yonenobu and Takenaka found no significant radiocarbon difference from their comparison material. This did not support an anomaly in the tested material, but does not exclude every possible scenario. Atmospheric disruption and airburst remain the basic explanation; fusion is not an established Tunguska mechanism.

### Slide 36　When insect damage is suspected, what happens in comparison trees?

The comparison trees change which evidence to seek next. On the left, hosts decline while comparison trees show little change. Host-specific defoliation is a candidate, but host-specific site conditions, competition or age structure can also matter. On the right, both groups decline. Consider a shared driver such as drought without excluding coincident insect damage. These are conceptual alternatives, not measured curves or records of a particular outbreak. Nonhosts are not automatically perfect controls: species differ in climate response and rooting, and host defoliation can alter light and water available to neighbouring comparison trees. Check several individuals, comparable sites and pre-event common variation, and verify ring boundaries and dates independently.

> https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_other/rmrs_1985_swetnam_t001.pdf

### Slide 37　Connect ring evidence, damage records and comparison trees

First establish the affected organ and agent using feeding traces, insects or damage surveys. Next compare the recorded event timing with growth and anatomical changes. Responses can be delayed and recovery can vary; they need not occur in the same year. Finally inspect nonhosts or same-species comparison trees and consider weather and competition. Traumatic resin ducts are clues, not insect-specific proof. This framework guides further observation rather than presenting a completed attribution. Also consider whether surviving trees represent the former stand. For a research plan, state one observation expected to differ between insect damage and a shared environmental driver. This is a learning check; assessment remains based only on the final report.

## Slide 38　From a question to a plan or review

Specify the system, disturbance hypothesis and predicted evidence. Plan comparisons, sampling, dating and missing-data handling. Identify alternatives and evidence that could change the conclusion. Link the target, predicted observations, comparisons and additional evidence to produce a plan that can actually test the explanation.
