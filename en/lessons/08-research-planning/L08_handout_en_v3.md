# Lesson 8: Linking environment and history
90 minutes / introductory course / working draft

## Reading evidence from tree rings
Observation means an inspected feature such as a ring width or scar. Dating places a ring in a calendar year through crossdating and anchoring. Interpretation concerns events such as felling, use, disturbance or climate change. A secure ring date does not automatically determine when timber was felled or used.

Archaeologists examine contexts and documents; ecologists compare trees and scars; climatologists calibrate and test against climate observations. Before combining sources, check differences in time, location and season.

## Case: wooden containers and dated slips
A study of Nara containers compares ring dates with written dates on wooden slips. Distinguish the event recorded on a slip from the year of the last preserved ring. Examine association, preservation and the sequence of use and deposition.

The Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties publishes ring-width values, images and measurement paths. A paper and a public dataset do not necessarily contain the same specimens. Check specimen identifiers and archaeological context before linking them. Access routes appear at the end of this handout.

# Exercise A: three timber dates
All examples are fictional. A document refers to a building in 1210. Samples were taken from different parts of the present structure. Spend 8 minutes on supported claims, limits and further evidence.

Timber A: last preserved ring 1187, bark absent. Timber B: last preserved ring 1199, bark present. Timber C: last preserved ring 1213, bark absent.

1. What does each year represent? What can be said about felling and use?
2. How could each timber relate to the 1210 record?
3. How could repair, reuse, storage, the meaning of the record and dating error be compared?
4. What should be checked about position, joints, working traces, bark and outer wood?

## Hints
The number of outer rings lost from A and C is unknown, so the last preserved ring alone cannot determine the felling year. C formed a ring in 1213, prompting a reassessment of its relationship to the 1210 record. Later repair or additions are possibilities to evaluate using structure and context.

B formed a ring in 1199. Even with bark, inspect the outer ring and position within the ring before deciding felling season; the interval before use also remains to be considered. Timber dates alone do not fix a building's construction or repair date.

# Exercise B: a research plan or literature review
Spend 8 minutes: draft for 5, review for 2 and revise for 1. Choose one format and state the question, sources, comparison and limits.

## Research plan
Possible opening: “Were the timbers at a site associated with the building's construction or a later repair?” Specify the material, sampling and preservation, dating method and comparison evidence. What positions or working traces would be expected under a repair hypothesis? Compare another explanation. Identify alternatives if sampling is not possible.

This is an illustrative proposal, not a report of actual materials or findings. Do not present planned measurements or expected results as completed observations.

## Literature review
Possible opening: “How can the relationship between temperature and ring width change over time?” Find relevant papers and compare region, species, season, calibration and verification periods, results and limits. Do not count articles based on the same data as independent evidence. Consider differences in samples and methods when results disagree.

## Peer review
Do the sources address the question? Is there a competing explanation? What remains unresolved and what evidence could change the conclusion? Are figure sources, units and scope clear?

# Report and routes to evidence
Choose a research plan or a review of studies or cases. Write by hand during the final 45 minutes of Lesson 8 and submit in class. You may consult course materials and notes. There is no minimum page or reference count, and figures are optional. Link the question, materials and method, comparison, evidence, conclusion and limits. Identify any sources you use as clearly as you can. The report alone determines the course grade.

## Finding papers and data
Follow a DOI to the publisher's article page and check the authors, year, title, methods and figures. Full text is not always freely available. Check a library or an author-hosted copy; if needed, politely request a copy from the corresponding author. For practical steps see [Supplement BG06: accessing research papers](https://github.com/zooc-14calbp/tree-rings-environment-history/blob/main/en/supplements/BG06_research_papers_en_v1.pdf).

Identify the source and scope of figures and data. Distinguish measurements, synthetic data and conceptual diagrams. Check the conditions for using external images and collaborative research materials.
