### Slide 5　Why reconstruct past climate?

A few decades of observations can tell us little about rare conditions outside that window. Extending the record with evidence such as tree rings lets us compare recent changes with earlier variability. A reconstruction can also inform evaluation of climate processes and models once its region and season are specified.

### Slide 6　Weather and climate: conditions and long-term statistics

Weather describes atmospheric conditions at a place and time. Climate describes their statistics over a period, including variability and the frequency of extremes as well as averages. Standard climatological normals use thirty years, but that does not set the duration of every climate study. A tree-ring reconstruction must specify its region, season, and target variable.

### Slide 7　“Another hot day. Is that global warming?”

Consider the everyday comment, another hot day, that must be global warming. Today’s heat is a weather event. Global warming describes a long-term change, which one day alone cannot establish. Equally, one cold day does not refute warming. The left-hand schematic combines a slowly rising mean with much larger short-term fluctuations. These are illustrative curves, not observations. On the right we hold the heat threshold fixed. Shifting the distribution towards higher temperatures increases the fraction of days above that threshold. This simplified example holds the shape of the distribution constant, although variability can also change in reality. We sometimes model short-term variability statistically as noise, but circulation, clouds, ocean conditions and land processes still have physical causes. Event attribution uses observations and models to assess how human influence changed the probability or intensity of a particular event. In tree-ring research we likewise distinguish a single narrow ring from a sustained climatic change.

### Slide 8　Extreme events: rare relative to what?

An extreme is defined relative to location, season, duration, and reference conditions. JMA generally uses a once-in-thirty-years-or-rarer criterion for temperature and precipitation anomalies. International studies also use application-specific thresholds, such as quantiles. A statistical anomaly is a departure from a reference and need not be extreme. A return period is not a repeating schedule. Hazard rarity and disaster impact are also different concepts.

### Slide 9　Reading time series: means, oscillations and spread

When reading a time series, first ask what changes. Periodic variation repeats at approximately fixed intervals, whereas quasi-periodic variation has less regular timing or amplitude. A trend is a gradual change in the mean level. A regime shift is a relatively rapid transition between persistent states. It may involve changes in variability or spatial relationships as well as the mean. In the lower-right panel, the mean stays constant while the spread increases. Stationarity concerns more than the mean: variance and dependence across time also matter. Real series combine these features. A visible step also calls for checks on station moves or instruments. In ring-width series, age effects, canopy release and insect damage are additional possibilities.

### Slide 11　ENSO: from the tropical Pacific to Japan

ENSO stands for El Niño–Southern Oscillation, a coupled variation of the tropical Pacific Ocean and atmosphere. During El Niño, the central and eastern equatorial Pacific are warmer than usual. Convection shifts, changing atmospheric circulation. Effects on the jet stream and pressure systems near Japan illustrate teleconnection. La Niña has broadly opposite sea-surface temperature anomalies. Effects over Japan depend on season and region. El Niño is associated with a warmer-winter tendency. Summer effects involve changes in the Pacific high, but do not guarantee a cool summer throughout Japan. Background warming, other ocean regions and internal atmospheric variability also contribute. Trees respond to local temperature, moisture and sunlight rather than directly recording distant ocean temperature.

### Slide 12　AO: the Arctic Oscillation and cold-air outbreaks

The Arctic Oscillation, or AO, is an approximately annular pattern of opposing pressure anomalies over the Arctic and midlatitudes. In its positive phase, relatively low polar pressure and high midlatitude pressure accompany stronger westerlies and a tendency for cold air to remain farther north. In the negative phase these pressure anomalies broadly reverse, and southward outbreaks of cold air become more likely. AO is associated with winter temperatures in Japan, especially northern Japan. But a negative index does not guarantee cold weather there. The pathway of the outbreak, Eurasian circulation and blocking also matter. The diagram is conceptual, not a set of observed isobars or a forecast.

### Slide 13　PDO: a North Pacific sea-surface temperature pattern

The Pacific Decadal Oscillation, or PDO, describes a leading pattern of North Pacific sea-surface temperature anomalies. In the positive phase the interior North Pacific tends to be cooler than usual while the North American coastal region is warmer. The negative phase broadly reverses these signs. Warm and cool mean anomalies relative to the usual conditions at that location and season, rather than absolute temperature differences between regions. Decadal variability is prominent, but PDO is not a clock that reverses every ten years. Faster variability is also present. It combines several ocean and atmospheric processes and is not independent of ENSO. Around Japan it provides part of the context for North Pacific circulation and ocean conditions. A positive index cannot be translated directly into a hot Japan. Tree-ring applications still require a specific site, season and independent validation.

### Slide 14　Distinguishing ENSO, AO and PDO

Compare the three patterns by location and variable. ENSO couples tropical Pacific sea-surface temperature and atmospheric circulation, with prominent variability over several years. AO is a pressure pattern between the Arctic and midlatitudes, varying from daily to seasonal scales, with winter cold-air outbreaks as a familiar Japanese connection. PDO is a North Pacific sea-surface temperature pattern with prominent decadal variability, alongside shorter fluctuations. We cannot simply add these indices as separate independent causes. To connect them with tree rings, first test their influence on the climate of a specific site and season, then test how that local climate limits growth. We now return to calibration and to checking the relationship outside the period used to estimate it.

### Slide 15　From tree rings to large-scale climate

When rings correlate with a large-scale index, examine the intervening links: local climate to growth, and large-scale circulation to local climate. These relationships need not remain stable through time. A North American study explicitly examined multidecadal modulation of ENSO links with precipitation and tree growth. Test independent periods and sites rather than relying on calibration fit alone. Changing correlations can arise from teleconnection changes, tree-response changes, or data and processing issues. This motivates calibration, validation, nonstationarity, and the later discussion of divergence.

### Slide 16　Limiting factors: Liebig’s barrel

In Liebig's barrel, the shortest stave limits the water level. At a dry site, moisture can strongly constrain growth. At a cold site, temperature can play that role. The constraints can change by season, and several can act together. Use the barrel to frame hypotheses, then compare growth with observations.

### Slide 17　Choosing species and sites

Choose species and sites sensitive to the target climate. Record age, slope, soils, crown position and history. Replicate among trees and across nearby sites. A tree that is easy to measure is not necessarily a good recorder of the target climate. Elevation, soil moisture and competition can all matter. Replication helps distinguish a common signal from an individual history, while site records explain why neighbouring trees or sites may respond differently.

### Slide 18　Previous-year effects and seasonality

Stored resources, foliage and buds can affect next year's growth. Previous summer and current spring can act through different pathways. Consider biological seasons as well as annual means. The year assigned to a ring is not necessarily the only year influencing its growth. Conditions in the preceding season can affect stored resources or developing buds. Always identify whether a monthly climate variable belongs to the current or previous year. This distinction matters when fitting and interpreting a model.

### Slide 19　Which tree-ring properties to measure

Ring width measures radial wood increment. Density and anatomy can record different processes. Stable isotopes reflect physiology and water as well as climate. Different measurements provide different views of tree function. Combining them can help test an interpretation, but adding proxies does not automatically improve accuracy. Check units, formation season and non-climatic influences for each measurement before deciding how the records can be combined.

### Slide 21　Season selection and hypotheses

Define the season, region and climate variable. Searching many months or models can select chance correlations. Evaluate the selection procedure using independent periods. ### Slide 23　Checking instrumental climate data

Check distance, elevation and terrain between station and forest. Read metadata on missing values and observational changes. Distinguish observations, interpolated values and anomalies. The closest station is not always the most representative. A mountain forest can differ substantially from a lowland station. Missing climate values are not zeros. Record any interpolation or adjustment, together with the definition of anomalies and the reference period used to calculate them.

### Slide 24　Chronology signal and sample depth

Check dating and measurement quality. Multiple cores from one tree are not independent trees. Show annual sample depth and stability of the shared signal. A long chronology can have much weaker replication in its earliest years. A value supported by one tree is not equivalent to a value supported by many independent trees. Read sample depth alongside the climate curve and check whether the common signal remains stable as the available samples change.

### Slide 25　Detrending and retained variation

Choose how to handle age- and size-related trends. Flexible detrending can remove long climate variations. Test whether conclusions change under alternative processing. Detrending is not a perfect separation of climate from biology. A flexible curve can follow and remove slow climatic variation as well as age effects. Choose processing in relation to the timescales of the research question, and examine how alternative reasonable methods affect the result.

### Slide 26　Chronology length and long timescales

A long chronology may comprise many shorter individual series. Series length and detrending constrain recoverable slow variation. This is discussed as the segment length curse. A chronology can extend for many centuries while being built from much shorter segments. Treating those segments individually can constrain the recovery of slow variation. Dating length and climatic timescale are therefore different properties. The relevant question is which variations survive the chosen construction and detrending methods.

### Slide 31　Keeping verification independent

Season, variable and processing choices are part of model building. Adjusting after viewing test results calls for a new independent evaluation. Record the time split and search procedure. Fixing the regression coefficients is not enough if the season was already chosen using the entire record. Evaluate the procedure that selected the model as well as the final equation. An unsuccessful test is useful evidence about the limits of the model, not a result to conceal.

### Slide 33　Exercise A: estimates and errors

Predictions are 9.2, 10.0 and 10.8degrees Celsius. Errors are +0.2, −0.1 and −0.1degrees Celsius. Agreement in this small example is not a general accuracy claim. The mean signed error is zero here, but the individual errors are not zero. Positive and negative errors cancel. We need to examine both average bias and the size of year-to-year errors. A model can have little average bias while still making substantial mistakes in particular years.

### Slide 34　Correlation, bias and prediction error

Correlation measures shared variation. Bias is systematic over- or underestimation. RMSE is the square root of mean squared error, in degrees Celsius here. High correlation can coexist with a persistent temperature error. If every estimate is one degree too warm, the series can still rise and fall with observations. Squaring errors before averaging prevents positive and negative mistakes from cancelling. Correlation and prediction error answer different questions.

### Slide 35　Equal correlation with different bias

Model B adds 1degrees Celsius to every estimate from model A. The pattern is unchanged, but the errors grow. Use more than correlation to assess agreement. The two model curves move in parallel because model B simply adds a constant. Adding a constant does not alter correlation, but it does change bias relative to observations. Decide whether the research question concerns the timing of variation, the actual values, or both, and choose diagnostics accordingly.

Compare predictions and observations on the same axes. Two lines can have similar shapes while one is consistently higher. Check both agreement and error in years withheld from calibration.

### Slide 39　Changing responses through time

A relationship between growth and climate can weaken or change over time. We introduce divergence as a term associated with this problem. Possible influences include changes in moisture, pollution, or competition. Compare separate time windows and independent evidence to evaluate when the relationship holds.

### Slide 45　Spatial and seasonal scope

Do not equate one site's spring temperature with global annual temperature. Test spatial similarities and differences against observations. State region, elevation, season and proxy in the conclusion. Spring temperature in one region and global annual temperature are different targets. The area represented by a chronology needs testing, not assumption. Even a short conclusion should identify the location, season and measurement on which it is based, so readers can see where the inference applies.

### Slide 46　Extreme years and average relationships

A linear model may fail outside its calibration range. Consider growth cessation, absent rings and survival selection. Distinguish identifying an extreme year from estimating its magnitude. Extreme conditions can create biological limits or missing growth. Extending a straight line beyond the observed range may therefore be misleading. Evidence that a year was unusual is different from evidence for its exact temperature or rainfall magnitude. Examine whether the calibration includes comparable conditions.

### Slide 47　Reproducible analysis records

Retain data, units, missing values, processing and equations. Record calibration and verification periods and selection decisions. Label sources, periods and synthetic versus observed data. A reproducible account lets another person follow the analysis from its inputs. Excluded samples and unsuccessful periods are particularly important to document. Cite the dataset and the subset used as well as the paper, and preserve the decisions that affect the result.
