### Slide 5　Dates help us frame better questions

Imagine finding a beam in an old building. Would you like to know when the tree grew, when it was felled, when the building was constructed, or when a repair introduced that particular beam? Choose one of these questions before continuing. The same piece of wood can be relevant to several questions, but they require different supporting evidence. The screen presents three ways to frame a historical question. First, when did something happen? A date helps us compare an event with records elsewhere. Second, which event came first? If forest growth changed and land use changed, their order can help us decide which explanations deserve further investigation. Third, what can the evidence actually show? Suppose a change in climate and a repair to a building occurred around the same time. Their dates alone do not establish that the climate change caused the repair. We would also need evidence about damage, building use, or the reasons for the work. Tree rings can strengthen the timeline used to examine such explanations. Keep the date obtained from a sample separate from the interpretation built around that date.

### Slide 8　What can real wood samples tell us?

A building timber can preserve the formation years of the surviving wood. A living tree can preserve changes in growth or the date of a scar. Tree rings can also provide evidence about climate before instrumental observations. In each case, begin with the specimen and ask what it actually preserves.

### Slide 9　Relative and numerical ages

When discussing dates, distinguish a relationship in time from an age expressed numerically. The left side of the screen describes relative age. One item is older or younger than another. Layers in the ground can provide such evidence, but we must check whether they have been overturned or whether older material has been moved into a newer deposit. The right side describes numerical age. For example, a particular ring formed in thirteen hundred of the Common Era. A numerical date helps us compare records and examine time differences. The presence of a number does not remove the need to check the evidence and its interpretation. Tree-ring dating involves more than counting rings. Researchers compare sequences, such as patterns of wide and narrow rings, among trees. They investigate possible missing rings and incorrectly identified boundaries while establishing calendar dates. Later lessons will explain the method in detail. For now, understand that annual dating is a result of matching and checking evidence. Whenever you encounter a date, ask what it refers to, what supports it, and which time scale is being used.

### Slide 11　Choosing a clock for the sample

What event is recorded by a charred seed, a mineral, or buried sand? Compare half-life, radiocarbon, mineral and carbonate dating, and luminescence by what is measured and when the clock starts. By the end, you should be able to choose a candidate method by matching the material and dated event to the question.

### Slide 12　Three questions to ask about a dating method

Use three questions to organize the methods that follow. First, how is the age expressed? Layers or artifact types can establish a relative order. Other evidence can supply a year or a numerical date range. Second, what event does the measurement date? A timber ring can date the formation of the surviving wood. To connect it with felling or construction, examine its preservation and use. Third, what record does the method use? Tree rings supply a growth sequence. Radiocarbon uses decay. Luminescence uses an accumulated signal, and archaeomagnetism can use the record preserved during cooling. Ask these three questions for each method.

### Slide 13　Radioactive decay and half-life

The graph introduces radioactive decay. Some atomic nuclei change into other nuclei over time. In a large population, the proportion remaining follows a predictable pattern. A half-life is the time required for that population to decrease to half its initial size. The vertical axis shows the percentage remaining. The horizontal axis counts elapsed half-lives. A value of one on that axis does not mean one year. After one half-life, fifty percent remains. After two, twenty-five percent remains. After three, the remaining proportion is twelve and a half percent. Each interval halves the amount still present at its beginning. Suppose a quarter of the original amount remains. How many half-lives have elapsed? Take a moment to work through the sequence. The answer is two. This graph is an idealized teaching model. Applying the idea to a real sample requires additional information. We must establish an appropriate starting condition, consider whether material entered or left the sample, and identify the event that started the clock. Different isotopes have different half-lives, so methods suit different materials and time ranges. A wooden object, a volcanic mineral and a cave deposit cannot automatically share the same dating procedure. For now, concentrate on the assumptions needed to convert a measured proportion into elapsed time. The supplementary mathematics develops the equations step by step.

### Slide 14　What event can a charred seed help date?

The photograph shows charred maize from Ceibal. Radiocarbon dating measures carbon-14 in material of biological origin. To date human activity, consider the relationship between growth, charring, and the find context. Measurements from rings of known calendar age help establish the calibration curve. Use that curve to relate an unknown sample's result to possible calendar ages.

### Slide 15　Dating mineral cooling and carbonate growth

These methods suit materials quite different from wood. On the left, potassium argon and argon argon dating examine suitable volcanic minerals. They use the production of argon from a radioactive isotope of potassium. Interpretation depends on when the mineral began retaining the relevant record, and on its later thermal and chemical history. The age may relate to cooling through an appropriate condition, rather than every event in the rock's history. On the right, uranium thorium dating can investigate suitable carbonate deposits, including cave formations. It uses changes in the isotopic relationship between uranium and thorium. Initial contamination and later movement of elements require checks. Rather than memorizing the names, compare the sample and the event represented by each clock. Suppose a carbonate layer formed over an archaeological deposit containing a stone tool. Dating the carbonate directly concerns its formation. Using that result to constrain tool use requires evidence that the tool was already present and that the layers remain in their original relationship. Volcanic ash presents a similar interpretive question. A mineral's age and the deposition of ash at a site need not coincide if older ash was transported again. Careful records of sample position and stratigraphy connect a laboratory result to the history we want to reconstruct.

### Slide 16　Dating sand burial and the cooling of a kiln

Here we compare two further kinds of record. On the left is luminescence dating. Radiation creates stored energy in suitable minerals, such as quartz. Laboratory stimulation releases a measurable light signal. Sufficient exposure to daylight or sufficient heating can reduce an earlier signal, giving the clock a new starting condition. Imagine sand grains exposed to daylight and then buried. Researchers assess the signal accumulated since burial together with the surrounding radiation conditions. OSL stands for optically stimulated luminescence. TL stands for thermoluminescence, which uses heating during measurement. Researchers must check whether the earlier signal was adequately reset and whether grains with different histories became mixed. On the right is archaeomagnetism. Suitable minerals in a heated structure can acquire a record of Earth's magnetic field as they cool. Researchers compare that record with a regional reference curve. Under appropriate conditions, a kiln can therefore provide evidence about its last sufficient heating and cooling. That event need not be the kiln's construction. Later movement or tilting can affect interpretation, especially when direction supplies the evidence. Sand burial and the final firing of a kiln are different target events. When selecting a method, ask what established the record's starting point, and what allowed the record to survive.

### Slide 28　Specimens, research material, and historical evidence

These terms describe different roles. A specimen or sample is collected, observed, or measured, such as a wood block or increment core. Research material includes information and objects used in a study, such as photographs, measurements, plans, and papers. A historical source supplies evidence about the past. Some usages restrict the Japanese term for historical sources to written records. This course uses it broadly to include artifacts and building timbers. The same timber can therefore be both a measured specimen and evidence for a historical question.

### Slide 29　How calendars organize months and seasons

A historical month needs a calendar context. A solar calendar keeps the year aligned with the seasons. The Gregorian calendar is an example. A lunar calendar counts twelve months based on lunar phases. The Islamic calendar is an example. A lunisolar calendar uses lunar months and adds intercalary months to keep the seasons aligned. Historical Japanese calendars use this approach. Before interpreting a month in an old record, establish its calendar and the conversion to your comparison dates.

### Slide 30　Islamic calendar months move through the seasons

The upper bar represents a solar year of about three hundred sixty-five days. Twelve lunar months total about three hundred fifty-four days, about eleven fewer. An Islamic month therefore moves earlier in the solar year over successive years. Ramadan occurs in different seasons. The era starts with the year of the Hijra, corresponding to six hundred twenty-two C E. The different year lengths also matter in conversion. Check calendar rules and the local determination of the start of a month when comparing historical dates.

### Slide 31　AD/BC and CE/BCE use the same year numbers

A D stands for anno Domini, and B C for before Christ. C E stands for Common Era, and B C E for Before Common Era. The labels use the same year numbers. A D seven thirty and seven thirty C E identify the same year. In historical notation, one B C E is followed by one C E. Astronomical numbering includes year zero, corresponding to one B C E. Check which convention a research dataset uses. We now return to radiocarbon B P and calibrated calendar-age notation.

### Slide 32　Age notation and reference years

The first example is one thousand plus or minus thirty B P, a conventional radiocarbon age referenced to nineteen fifty. Use a calibration curve to express it in calendar time. Cal B P also uses nineteen fifty as its reference. The ring year thirteen hundred C E is an example of a directly assigned calendar year. Always state which kind of age you are reporting.
