### Slide 6　A tree is a connected living organism

Leaves: photosynthesis and gas exchange. Stem: support, transport and storage. Roots: water and nutrient uptake. Photosynthesis, water uptake and stem transport are connected. Interpret stem growth as part of a living organism rather than an isolated clock.

### Slide 7　Growth in length and thickness

Primary growth Apical meristems extend shoots and roots. Secondary growth Cambial activity adds wood and thickness. Growth in length and growth in thickness involve different meristems and directions. Keep their positions distinct when reading the diagram.

### Slide 8　From stem to cambium: where is new wood added?

The vascular cambium lies between phloem and xylem. It produces secondary xylem inward and secondary phloem outward. New wood lies outside older wood, not at the pith. Tissue widths are schematic. Upper stem portions formed later, so ring counts also depend on sampling height.

### Slide 9　Wood also contains living cells

Tissue or cell. Typical state and function at maturity. Tracheids / vessel elements. Usually dead; involved in water conduction and support. Sapwood parenchyma. Includes living cells for storage and metabolism. Cambial cells. Living meristem supplying xylem and phloem cells. Wood contains cell types with different functions and lifetimes. Distinguish mature conducting cells from living parenchyma rather than treating all wood alike.

### Slide 10　Sapwood and heartwood

Sapwood Outer wood involved in water transport and storage. Heartwood Inner, transformed wood, usually no longer conducting water. A colour change is not itself a growth-ring boundary. The sapwood-heartwood boundary is different from an annual growth boundary. Colour alone does not establish the year a tree was felled.

### Slide 11　Ring counts depend on sampling height

Upper stem sections form later. A higher sample can contain a shorter record. Distinguish a ring count from age since germination. A tree takes time to reach the sampling height. The ring count at that height alone does not measure the full time since germination.

### Slide 12　Cell-development phases overlap within a season

The horizontal axis represents seasonal progression. Bands illustrate overlap, not measurements. A cell progresses through enlargement, wall formation and maturation, but different cell cohorts occupy different phases at the same time. Wall formation may continue after cambial division stops. Ring width therefore cannot identify a single formation date.

### Slide 13　Separate cell enlargement from wall formation

Follow a simplified tracheid through development. Division produces a new cell; enlargement increases its dimensions. Secondary-wall material is deposited inward and lignification proceeds. Maturation removes the cell contents, leaving the lumen and wall. Distinguish enlargement of the outline from thickening of the wall. Phases partly overlap, and neighbouring cells may differ in stage. Living sapwood parenchyma does not share this same fate.

### Slide 14　Identifying planes and cells

Why does the same piece of wood look different when cut in another plane? Identify the three planes and compare tracheids and vessels in conifer, ring-porous, and diffuse-porous wood. By the end, you should be able to use the cutting plane and cell arrangement to explain the visible anatomy.

### Slide 15　Check the three wood directions in 3D

View wood as a small three-dimensional block. L follows the stem axis, R points from pith towards bark, and T is tangent to a growth ring. The rectangular block simplifies local directions in a small wood sample. Its top is transverse to L. The front face follows L and R, while the side face follows L and T. R and T vary in orientation around the stem. Distinguish directions from planes, then compare what each plane reveals.

> https://research.fs.usda.gov/fpl/wood-handbook

### Slide 16　Three planes reveal the same tissue differently

Section orientation changes the appearance of the same tissue. A transverse section cuts across cells elongated along the stem, exposing their lumens. A radial section follows L and R and can show rays along their radial extent. A tangential section follows L and T and cuts across those rays. These cells and growth-layer lines are schematic relationships, not micrographs or measured anatomy of a particular species. Think about which cells are elongated in which direction and how the section intersects them.

> https://research.fs.usda.gov/fpl/wood-handbook

### Slide 17　Tracheids and vessels: where does water pass?

Tracheids overlap, and water moves between neighbouring cells through pits in their side walls. A pit retains a pit membrane; it is not simply an open hole. Vessel elements join end to end through perforations, forming vessels; their side walls also contain pits. Compare connectivity, not dimensions. The following transverse photographs view these cells end-on.

### Slide 22　Tracing ring boundaries

Which visible line on a cross-section marks the boundary between years? Examine earlywood and latewood, false rings, and discontinuous rings in diagrams and photographs. By the end, you should be able to use changes in cells and continuity across the surface to flag uncertain boundaries.

### Slide 23　A ring is an increment of wood

A yearly increment includes earlywood and latewood. A tree-ring boundary separates adjacent annual increments. A dark band alone is not the whole yearly increment. Counting thin lines alone can miss important structure. Think of a ring as a band of wood with width and internal anatomical variation.

This Scots pine image is published as an example of a discontinuous ring. The two vertical guides mark different approximately radial observation paths. They are not established ring-boundary traces. ### Slide 32　What can 42 rings tell us?

At 1.3 m: bark present, pith missing. 42 visible rings. This does not establish 42 years since germination. Assess missing inner wood, years to reach height, annuality and absent rings. Sampling height and missing pith affect the relation between ring count and age. State those conditions when interpreting a count as age evidence.

### Slide 33　Summary

The cambium adds xylem inward. Wood contains cells involved in transport, support and storage. Read boundaries from anatomy. Use criteria appropriate to the wood type and plane. A count alone does not fix a date. Consider sample position, missing wood and ring anomalies. Connecting cambial position, wood anatomy and boundary decisions allows you to explain what a measured ring width actually represents.
