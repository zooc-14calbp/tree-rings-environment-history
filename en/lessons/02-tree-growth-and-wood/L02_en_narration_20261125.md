### Slide 6　A tree is a connected living organism

Leaves: photosynthesis and gas exchange. Stem: support, transport and storage. Roots: water and nutrient uptake. Photosynthesis, water uptake and stem transport are connected. Interpret stem growth as part of a living organism rather than an isolated clock.

### Slide 7　Growth in length and thickness

Primary growth Apical meristems extend shoots and roots. Secondary growth Cambial activity adds wood and thickness. Growth in length and growth in thickness involve different meristems and directions. Keep their positions distinct when reading the diagram.

### Slide 8　Conifer form: a leader and lateral branches

Many conifers maintain a strong main axis, with a leader extending above lateral branches. These three stages represent one schematic tree, not a particular species or specified ages. Extension takes place near shoot tips, while secondary growth thickens stems and branches. Existing stem segments do not stretch like rubber. The orange marks identify the same branch attachment: height growth does not carry that attachment upward by stretching the older trunk. Lean, branch orientation and the ground surface can nevertheless change. A persistent main axis illustrates monopodial branching. Apical dominance alone does not explain every aspect of crown architecture. Species, light, competition and leader damage all influence form, and conifers do not universally develop identical conical crowns. Lower-branch death can raise the base of the living crown without moving the older branch attachments upward.

### Slide 9　Growth curves: size versus annual increment

The left panel shows modelled height against tree age. The right panel takes one-year differences from that very same curve. These are illustrative model values, not measurements of Japanese cedar or another species. The left panel describes accumulated size; the right describes the amount added during a year. Where the height curve is steepest, the annual increment is largest. Height can continue increasing while annual height increment becomes smaller. A sigmoid curve is one basic description of tree growth, but species and site alter its form, and suppression, release or leader damage can interrupt the pattern. Height, diameter, volume and mass are distinct quantities. Slower height extension or narrower rings cannot by themselves establish that whole-tree mass accumulation has stopped. This illustrative curve also cannot determine the age of an unknown tree.

### Slide 10　Representative deterministic growth curves

We compare logistic, Gompertz and Chapman–Richards growth curves. Deterministic means that time and parameters specify a unique predicted value, not that a tree’s future is perfectly predetermined. These illustrative curves share an asymptote of thirty-five metres but use arbitrary parameter choices. Their initial values, rise and curvature differ. No measured data were used to judge which model fits best. In this parameterization, logistic and Gompertz curves have positive values at time zero; that origin must not be interpreted automatically as germination. Select a form and parameters appropriate to the species, site and interval, and examine residuals and predictions for other periods. Smooth curves alone do not explain all annual responses to weather or disturbance. The supplement gives equations and rates, and distinguishes growth through time from allometry linking quantities such as diameter and biomass.

### Slide 11　Equal area increments can produce narrower rings

The two circular stem sections are shown at the same scale, with initial wood radii of five and fifteen centimetres. Each orange annulus adds twenty square centimetres. The smaller stem gains a ring about six millimetres wide, whereas the larger stem gains only about 2.1 millimetres. The curve starts with a five-centimetre radius and adds the same area annually. Its vertical axis uses base-ten logarithmic spacing labelled in the original millimetres, so equal ratios occupy equal distances. Rings narrow without any imposed climate deterioration. Basal area increment, or BAI, describes sectional area added, not whole-stem volume or whole-tree mass increment. Real stems can be eccentric and contain discontinuous rings, so a single radial series may not establish the actual area increment accurately. Chapter 4 connects this geometry to separating size- or age-related trends from the variation of interest.

### Slide 12　From stem to cambium: where is new wood added?

The vascular cambium lies between phloem and xylem. It produces secondary xylem inward and secondary phloem outward. New wood lies outside older wood, not at the pith. Tissue widths are schematic. Upper stem portions formed later, so ring counts also depend on sampling height.

### Slide 13　Wood also contains living cells

Tissue or cell. Typical state and function at maturity. Tracheids / vessel elements. Usually dead; involved in water conduction and support. Sapwood parenchyma. Includes living cells for storage and metabolism. Cambial cells. Living meristem supplying xylem and phloem cells. Wood contains cell types with different functions and lifetimes. Distinguish mature conducting cells from living parenchyma rather than treating all wood alike.

### Slide 14　Sapwood and heartwood

Sapwood Outer wood involved in water transport and storage. Heartwood Inner, transformed wood, usually no longer conducting water. A colour change is not itself a growth-ring boundary. The sapwood-heartwood boundary is different from an annual growth boundary. Colour alone does not establish the year a tree was felled.

### Slide 15　Ring counts depend on sampling height

Upper stem sections form later. A higher sample can contain a shorter record. Distinguish a ring count from age since germination. A tree takes time to reach the sampling height. The ring count at that height alone does not measure the full time since germination.

### Slide 16　Cell-development phases overlap within a season

The horizontal axis represents seasonal progression. Bands illustrate overlap, not measurements. A cell progresses through enlargement, wall formation and maturation, but different cell cohorts occupy different phases at the same time. Wall formation may continue after cambial division stops. Ring width therefore cannot identify a single formation date.

### Slide 17　Separate cell enlargement from wall formation

Follow a simplified tracheid through development. Division produces a new cell; enlargement increases its dimensions. Secondary-wall material is deposited inward and lignification proceeds. Maturation removes the cell contents, leaving the lumen and wall. Distinguish enlargement of the outline from thickening of the wall. Phases partly overlap, and neighbouring cells may differ in stage. Living sapwood parenchyma does not share this same fate.

### Slide 20　Cut orientation changes growth-ring figure and ray appearance

Growth rings appear as arcs in transverse view, with rays running outwards from the pith. Radial sections commonly show roughly parallel, quarter-sawn figure and expose rays along their radial extent. Tangential cuts can produce cathedral-like figure where curved or inclined growth-layer surfaces intersect the cutting plane. A cathedral pattern is not inevitable: straight cylindrical layers can give parallel traces on a longitudinal tangential plane. Blue marks emphasize rays. They appear elongated across the radial face but as small sections when cut tangentially. Their size and spacing are exaggerated and do not represent a micrograph at the same magnification as the brown figure. Connect these views with the real tissue photographs that follow. A transverse face cuts across longitudinal cells, whereas both longitudinal sections can show those cells along their length.

### Slide 21　Tracheids and vessels: where does water pass?

Tracheids overlap, and water moves between neighbouring cells through pits in their side walls. A pit retains a pit membrane; it is not simply an open hole. Vessel elements join end to end through perforations, forming vessels; their side walls also contain pits. Compare connectivity, not dimensions. The following transverse photographs view these cells end-on.

### Slide 26　Tracing ring boundaries

Which visible line on a cross-section marks the boundary between years? Examine earlywood and latewood, false rings, and discontinuous rings in diagrams and photographs. By the end, you should be able to use changes in cells and continuity across the surface to flag uncertain boundaries.

### Slide 27　A ring is an increment of wood

A yearly increment includes earlywood and latewood. A tree-ring boundary separates adjacent annual increments. A dark band alone is not the whole yearly increment. Counting thin lines alone can miss important structure. Think of a ring as a band of wood with width and internal anatomical variation.

### Slide 37　Summary

The cambium adds xylem inward. Wood contains cells involved in transport, support and storage. Read boundaries from anatomy. Use criteria appropriate to the wood type and plane.
