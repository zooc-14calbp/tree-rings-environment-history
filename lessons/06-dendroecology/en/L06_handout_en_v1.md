# Lesson 6: Dendroecology — handout
Reading Environment and History through Tree Rings | 90 minutes | Draft v1 | 30 October 2026

## Central question
Can a tree preserve evidence that insects ate its leaves? What survives in growth, anatomy, injuries and population ages? Explain dendroecological questions and distinguish observed changes from hypotheses about their causes.

## Combine three types of evidence
1. Growth: timing, magnitude, duration and synchrony of suppression or release.
2. Injuries: crossdated scars, their likely causes and preservation.
3. Populations: establishment, regeneration and mortality, including missing individuals.

## Interpreting causes
A canopy gap changes light and competition. But growth increases can also reflect climate, water availability, recovery from insect damage or management. A single wide ring or percentage change does not establish a cause. Responses may lag behind events.

Comparison stands and non-host species can help test hypotheses. Differences in species, site, size and management remain: comparison trees are not automatically experimental controls. Consider dead wood, stumps, regeneration, inventories, photographs and damage records as well.

## Age and growth measures
A first preserved ring at sampling height in 1980 does not prove germination in 1980. Consider missing pith and the time needed to reach that height. Diameter alone is a poor age indicator, and surviving trees do not represent all past individuals.

For an ideal circular stem, basal area increment (BAI) = π[(r + w)² − r²], where r is previous radius and w is current ring width. With w = 1 mm, r = 10 mm adds about 66 mm², whereas r = 30 mm adds about 192 mm². Radius, stem form and sampling height introduce uncertainties. BAI alone does not determine biomass or carbon.

## Limits of interpretation
Do not substitute zero for not recording. Even an eligible tree need not be scarred by a fire. The table contains selected fictional dates: intervals between them do not necessarily represent all fires. Scarred-tree percentages in this sample are not burned-area percentages or fire severity.

## Explore actual records
NOAA’s International Multiproxy Paleofire Database (IMPD; R37) includes different fire-history evidence, including scars and charcoal. Check recording-period and code definitions before analysis. R38 provides giant-sequoia examples; R40 combines different evidence for postfire forest development.

# Terms and brief explanations
| Term | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| dendroecology | Studying ecological processes and forest history with dated rings. |
| stand dynamics | Changes in growth, recruitment and mortality within a stand. |
| canopy gap | An opening in the forest canopy. |
| disturbance | An event that changes ecosystem structure or resource conditions. |
| growth suppression | A period of relatively slow growth. |
| growth release | A sustained growth increase; operational criteria must be stated. |
| basal area increment (BAI) | Annual increase in stem cross-sectional area, not biomass itself. |
| regeneration | Forest renewal involving the appearance and growth of young trees. |
| establishment | A tree becoming established; distinct from the first ring at sampling height. |
| survivorship bias | Bias caused by considering survivors alone. |
| fire scar | Evidence left by fire damage to cambium and subsequent growth. |
| recording tree | A tree considered capable of recording and preserving a scar at that time. |
| defoliation | Loss of foliage, for example through insect feeding. |
| dendroentomology | Using tree rings to investigate insect histories. |
| intra-annual density fluctuations (IADFs) | Density or anatomical variations within a ring; not uniquely diagnostic of insects. |

A scar-recording period, the measured ring interval and the tree’s lifetime are not necessarily identical. Separate what can be observed from what actually occurred.

# Research examples, links and assignment
## Read insect evidence in widths and anatomy
Khasanov and Sandlersky (2018; R42) compared 49 oaks with 21 non-host ashes, examining intra-annual density fluctuations (IADFs) and abrupt growth changes. Ask what anatomy and the non-host comparison add beyond narrow rings alone. IADFs are not uniquely diagnostic of insect damage. The lesson uses a study-design summary; anatomical images will be selected later.

## Other research examples
Cockchafer-related periodic narrow rings can complicate crossdating (R41). Check anatomy, damage records and independent dating evidence instead of relying on periodic resemblance. The slide’s index series is synthetic.

Hirano and Morimoto (1999; R39) compared polluted and unpolluted Japanese-black-pine stands. Read how a climate model and SO₂ records support their interpretation.

# Further cases: survivors, eruptions and earthquakes
## Several events in one pine
The USGS specimen account (R43) combines scars, staining and death in Pinus albicaulis. Consider drought, bark beetles and fungi together; the ultimate single cause of death remains unresolved. Follow the original image link; a teaching-image replacement will be considered later.

## Hemlocks that survived infestation
Walker et al. (2014; R45) studied surviving trees at six sites: three declined, while the others showed no change or increased growth. The Virginia Tech story (R44) leads to the primary study. Discuss survivorship bias and alternatives such as changing competition.

## Mount St. Helens
Use the independently dated 18 May 1980 eruption to examine spatial damage and survival, then suppression, scars, mortality and establishment. Dendrovolcanology combines tree evidence with deposits and topography. Distinguish this known event from reconstruction of earlier eruption dates.

## The San Andreas fault
At Wrightwood, suppression from 1813 was linked with the late-1812 earthquake (R47–R49). Dendroseismology compares off-fault trees, sediments and historical records. Separate first growth response from event timing. This connects the lesson to dendrogeomorphology.
