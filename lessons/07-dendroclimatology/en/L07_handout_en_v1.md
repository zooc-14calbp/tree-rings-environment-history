# Lesson 7: Dendroclimatology - handout
Reading Environment and History through Tree Rings / 90 minutes / Draft / 30 October 2026

## Define the reconstruction target
Dendroclimatology relates tree-ring measurements to instrumental climate and estimates earlier conditions. Secure calendar dating does not automatically establish accurate climate estimates. Define region, season, variable and units before interpreting a curve.

## Biological conditions
Responses depend on limiting temperature or moisture, species, soils and crown position. Stored resources and buds can carry previous-year effects into later growth. Consider seasons and mechanisms rather than only a correlation with annual averages. Competition, insects, pollution and management remain relevant alternatives from Lesson 6.

## Chronology and instrumental observations
Check boundaries, measurements and crossdating. Distinguish cores from independent trees and report annual sample depth, stability of the common signal and weaker early replication. For climate observations, examine station location, elevation, terrain, missing data, observational changes and adjustments.

## Detrending and timescale
A flexible reference curve can remove slow climatic variation as well as age trends. A long chronology assembled from short individual series can face limits in recovering slow variation, discussed as the segment length curse. Test whether processing retains the variation required by the question.

# Exercise A: calibration and verification
8 minutes: calculate 4, compare 3, record 1. All data are synthetic and do not demonstrate the accuracy of a real reconstruction.

Calibration uses 2001–2004. Ring indices I are 0.7, 0.9, 1.1 and 1.3; temperatures are 8.9, 9.5, 10.3 and 11.3°C. These teaching values give the equation T_est = 6 + 4I. I is dimensionless and T is a seasonal mean temperature in °C. Do not transfer the coefficients to another forest.

# Exercise B: compare correlation and error
6 minutes. Model A is T_est = 6 + 4I. Model B adds 1°C to every A estimate. Compare both with a constant prediction of 10°C.

| Year | Observed (°C) | A (°C) | B (°C) | Baseline (°C) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2005 | 9.0 | 9.2 | 10.2 | 10.0 |
| 2006 | 10.1 | 10.0 | 11.0 | 10.0 |
| 2007 | 10.9 | 10.8 | 11.8 | 10.0 |

## Calculation
For root mean squared error, square the errors, average them and take the square root. The unit here is °C. Work through those three steps for each prediction.

<!-- writing-space -->
## Interpretation
1. Correlation measures shared variation.
2. Bias is systematic over- or underestimation.
3. Adding a constant preserves correlation but changes prediction error.
4. Check improvement over a simple constant prediction too.

Do not generalize real-model performance from these three years. Research also examines autocorrelation, searching across candidates and changing seasonal or temporal responses. Mathematical supplement BG03 develops the equations further.

# A Japanese case and response stability
## Hinoki and early spring temperature
Yonenobu and Eckstein (2006) examined February–April temperature in central Japan using Hinoki ring widths. Values below are reported in Table 1, not recalculated for this course.

| Stage | Period | Correlation r |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calibration | 1900–1961 | 0.514 |
| Verification | 1962–2001 | 0.078 |

The later loss of predictive skill illustrates why a successful calibration need not remain valid indefinitely. The paper discusses possible explanations including pollution, without establishing one cause for every mismatch. Compare observations, forest history and other proxies.

## Beyond width
Nakatsuka and colleagues (2020) integrate oxygen and hydrogen isotope information to examine summer climate in central Japan. Physiology and tree age still affect isotopes; they are not direct rainfall measurements.

## Reading questions
Which region and season are represented? Which periods were used for calibration and independent verification? Are unsuccessful periods reported? Is a proposed cause a hypothesis or a conclusion supported by independent evidence?

# Uncertainty and the scope of a conclusion
## Read the figure
The 1800–1810 slide curve is synthetic. Its bounds add and subtract a chosen 0.8°C and are not statistical 95% intervals. In real figures, check the interval's meaning, calculation, assumptions and omissions.

## Separate uncertainty sources
Residual variation, coefficient estimation, observational error, chronology quality, declining replication, response change and processing choices are distinct issues. Secure calendar dating does not eliminate uncertainty in the climate variable.

## Region, season and extremes
One site's spring temperature differs from global annual temperature. Test spatial correspondence with observations. Outside the calibration range, consider growth limits and absent rings. Identifying an extreme year differs from estimating its magnitude.

## Retain records
Keep data, identifiers, units, missing values, settings, equations, seasonal choices, time splits and reasons for exclusions.

## Exit questions
1. Explain how high correlation can coexist with bias.
2. Why independently evaluate season selection as well as coefficients?
3. What information must accompany a reconstruction curve?

# Terms and further reading

| Term | Brief explanation |
| --- | --- |
| dendroclimatology | Estimating past climate using tested tree-ring relationships. |
| calibration | Estimating a model from paired observations. |
| verification | Evaluating against information unused in building the model. |
| bias | Systematic over- or underestimation. |
| autocorrelation | Association between time-separated values in one series. |
| extrapolation | Estimation beyond the range over which a model was tested. |
