## 1. Dendroclimatology

Lesson 7, 90 minutes. Testing climate evidence. This lesson examines climate reconstruction from tree rings. Assigning a ring to a calendar year does not by itself determine that year's temperature or rainfall. We will define the target climate, build a relationship with instrumental measurements, and test whether that relationship predicts observations it has not seen. This video contains the explanations for a ninety-minute classroom lesson. Pause to complete the exercises.

## 2. Learning objectives

Relate climate responses to species, site and season. Distinguish calibration from independent verification. Explain the scope and limits of a reconstruction. Our calculation uses a simple linear equation, but the main issue is where the equation applies. A good fit in the period used to estimate its coefficients is only a first step. We also need independent testing, biological interpretation, and attention to the disturbances discussed in Lesson 6.

## 3. Crossdating and climate reconstruction

Crossdating assigns calendar years to rings. Climate reconstruction needs a relationship with a defined climate variable. Accurate dates alone do not guarantee accurate temperatures. Dating establishes which observations belong together in time. Interpretation then asks why growth changed. A shared narrow ring may reflect climate, but it can also involve ecological stress. Keep the evidence for the date separate from the evidence for a particular climate interpretation.

## 4. Limiting factors and climate sensitivity

Some sites are temperature limited, others water limited. The growth effect of warming differs among sites. Consider photosynthesis, respiration, water transport and cambial activity. Warmer conditions can extend growth where low temperature is limiting. At a dry site, the same warming can increase water stress. The sign of a correlation therefore needs a biological explanation. Site conditions determine which mechanism is plausible, rather than a universal rule that wider rings always mean warmer years.

## 5. Choosing species and sites

Choose species and sites sensitive to the target climate. Record age, slope, soils, crown position and history. Replicate among trees and across nearby sites. A tree that is easy to measure is not necessarily a good recorder of the target climate. Elevation, soil moisture and competition can all matter. Replication helps distinguish a common signal from an individual history, while site records explain why neighbouring trees or sites may respond differently.

## 6. Previous-year effects and seasonality

Stored resources, foliage and buds can affect next year's growth. Previous summer and current spring can act through different pathways. Consider biological seasons as well as annual means. The year assigned to a ring is not necessarily the only year influencing its growth. Conditions in the preceding season can affect stored resources or developing buds. Always identify whether a monthly climate variable belongs to the current or previous year. This distinction matters when fitting and interpreting a model.

## 7. Which tree-ring properties to measure

Ring width measures radial wood increment. Density and anatomy can record different processes. Stable isotopes reflect physiology and water as well as climate. Different measurements provide different views of tree function. Combining them can help test an interpretation, but adding proxies does not automatically improve accuracy. Check units, formation season and non-climatic influences for each measurement before deciding how the records can be combined.

## 9. Season selection and hypotheses

Define the season, region and climate variable. Searching many months or models can select chance correlations. Evaluate the selection procedure using independent periods. Exploration is useful, but an exploratory result is not independent verification. If the test period is used to choose a season or model, it has become part of model selection. Document when each decision was made and reserve genuinely unused observations for evaluating the complete procedure.

## 10. Checking instrumental climate data

Check distance, elevation and terrain between station and forest. Read metadata on missing values and observational changes. Distinguish observations, interpolated values and anomalies. The closest station is not always the most representative. A mountain forest can differ substantially from a lowland station. Missing climate values are not zeros. Record any interpolation or adjustment, together with the definition of anomalies and the reference period used to calculate them.

## 11. Chronology signal and sample depth

Check dating and measurement quality. Multiple cores from one tree are not independent trees. Show annual sample depth and stability of the shared signal. A long chronology can have much weaker replication in its earliest years. A value supported by one tree is not equivalent to a value supported by many independent trees. Read sample depth alongside the climate curve and check whether the common signal remains stable as the available samples change.

## 12. Detrending and retained variation

Choose how to handle age- and size-related trends. Flexible detrending can remove long climate variations. Test whether conclusions change under alternative processing. Detrending is not a perfect separation of climate from biology. A flexible curve can follow and remove slow climatic variation as well as age effects. Choose processing in relation to the timescales of the research question, and examine how alternative reasonable methods affect the result.

## 13. Chronology length and long timescales

A long chronology may comprise many shorter individual series. Series length and detrending constrain recoverable slow variation. This is discussed as the segment length curse. A chronology can extend for many centuries while being built from much shorter segments. Treating those segments individually can constrain the recovery of slow variation. Dating length and climatic timescale are therefore different properties. The relevant question is which variations survive the chosen construction and detrending methods.

These coefficients belong only to this synthetic example. An index of one gives an estimated temperature of ten degrees Celsius. An increase of one tenth in the index changes the estimate by four tenths of a degree. This is not an equation for raw widths in millimetres. Changing the predictor definition or standardization changes the model to which the coefficients belong.

## 17. Keeping verification independent

Season, variable and processing choices are part of model building. Adjusting after viewing test results calls for a new independent evaluation. Record the time split and search procedure. Fixing the regression coefficients is not enough if the season was already chosen using the entire record. Evaluate the procedure that selected the model as well as the final equation. An unsuccessful test is useful evidence about the limits of the model, not a result to conceal.

## 19. Exercise A: estimates and errors

Predictions are 9.2, 10.0 and 10.8degrees Celsius. Errors are +0.2, −0.1 and −0.1degrees Celsius. Agreement in this small example is not a general accuracy claim. The mean signed error is zero here, but the individual errors are not zero. Positive and negative errors cancel. We need to examine both average bias and the size of year-to-year errors. A model can have little average bias while still making substantial mistakes in particular years.

## 20. Correlation, bias and prediction error

Correlation measures shared variation. Bias is systematic over- or underestimation. RMSE is the square root of mean squared error, in degrees Celsius here. High correlation can coexist with a persistent temperature error. If every estimate is one degree too warm, the series can still rise and fall with observations. Squaring errors before averaging prevents positive and negative mistakes from cancelling. Correlation and prediction error answer different questions.

## 21. Equal correlation with different bias

Model B adds 1degrees Celsius to every estimate from model A. The pattern is unchanged, but the errors grow. Use more than correlation to assess agreement. The two model curves move in parallel because model B simply adds a constant. Adding a constant does not alter correlation, but it does change bias relative to observations. Decide whether the research question concerns the timing of variation, the actual values, or both, and choose diagnostics accordingly.

## 24. Changing responses through time

Growth-climate relationships may change through time. Reconsider water stress, pollution, competition and physiology. Use moving periods and independent evidence to assess stability. A reconstruction assumes that the relationship found in the observational period remains useful outside it. That assumption needs evidence. Changes in limiting factors or forest history can introduce bias. Compare periods and independent records rather than assigning every mismatch to a single cause.

## 26. Summer climate from stable isotopes

Cellulose oxygen isotopes reflect source water and leaf processes. A central Japan study integrates oxygen and hydrogen isotopes. Check age and physiological effects in isotope records too. Isotopes provide information different from ring width, but they are not direct rain gauges free of physiological influences. The study by Nakatsuka and colleagues combines oxygen and hydrogen information to examine climate and physiological components. Interpret the measured quantity before translating it into a climatic story.

## 27. Reconstruction curves and uncertainty

Show uncertainty and sample depth alongside the curve. This fictional illustration is not a 95% interval. Distinguish model error, changing response and chronology quality. The upper and lower curves are illustrative bounds chosen to explain uncertainty. They are not calculated confidence or prediction intervals. In a real reconstruction, state what uncertainty an interval represents and how it was estimated. Several important sources of uncertainty may require more than a single band.

## 28. Spatial and seasonal scope

Do not equate one site's spring temperature with global annual temperature. Test spatial similarities and differences against observations. State region, elevation, season and proxy in the conclusion. Spring temperature in one region and global annual temperature are different targets. The area represented by a chronology needs testing, not assumption. Even a short conclusion should identify the location, season and measurement on which it is based, so readers can see where the inference applies.

## 29. Extreme years and average relationships

A linear model may fail outside its calibration range. Consider growth cessation, absent rings and survival selection. Distinguish identifying an extreme year from estimating its magnitude. Extreme conditions can create biological limits or missing growth. Extending a straight line beyond the observed range may therefore be misleading. Evidence that a year was unusual is different from evidence for its exact temperature or rainfall magnitude. Examine whether the calibration includes comparable conditions.

## 30. Reproducible analysis records

Retain data, units, missing values, processing and equations. Record calibration and verification periods and selection decisions. Label sources, periods and synthetic versus observed data. A reproducible account lets another person follow the analysis from its inputs. Excluded samples and unsuccessful periods are particularly important to document. Cite the dataset and the subset used as well as the paper, and preserve the decisions that affect the result.

## 31. Exit questions

Does high correlation imply little bias?. Why must season selection be separated from verification?. What information should accompany a reconstructed curve?. Answer each question briefly and identify a figure or calculation supporting the answer. The key distinctions are correlation versus error, selection versus verification, and a reconstructed curve versus the uncertainty surrounding it. Use those distinctions when reading a published reconstruction.
