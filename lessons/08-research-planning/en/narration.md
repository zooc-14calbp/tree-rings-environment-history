## 1. Research plans linking environment and history

Lesson 8, 90 minutes. Combining tree-ring evidence. The final lesson turns the methods into a research plan. Measuring rings, assigning dates, interpreting environmental conditions and explaining human activities are connected tasks. Each connection needs its own supporting evidence. We will practise making those steps explicit rather than jumping from a date to a complete historical explanation. This video contains the explanations for a ninety-minute classroom lesson. Pause to complete the exercises.

## 2. Learning objectives

Separate evidence for dates, environment and causes. Interpret agreement and disagreement among sources. Develop a testable question and an evidence plan. There are two exercises. First we interpret several dating clues without claiming more than they support. Then we turn a personal area of interest into a plan with a defined target, suitable materials, an analysis, and alternative explanations that can be tested.

## 3. Three applications and a shared procedure

Archaeology asks about objects, buildings and human activity. Ecology asks about changes in trees and stands. Climatology asks about defined regional and seasonal climate. The applications share recording, dating, comparisons and explicit limits, but they ask different questions. Strong evidence for an object's age is not automatically evidence for a climate event or a social cause. Identify what each observation supports before combining it with another field.

## 4. Observation, dating and interpretation

Observation: directly measured or inspected features. Dating: placement by crossdating and calendar anchoring. Interpretation: inference about cutting, disturbance, climate or activity. Bark preservation is an observation. Assigning the adjacent ring to a year is a dating result. Inferring felling or construction adds assumptions about preservation, processing and use. Writing these as separate statements is a practical way to detect gaps in the reasoning.

## 5. Wood and archaeological time

Ring formation, felling, use and deposition are different events. Reuse and repair can separate timber and building dates. Check the evidence connecting these stages. This diagram shows stages rather than dates from a particular site. A lower bound for one stage does not specify the length of the next interval. Avoid silently assuming that felling, manufacture, use and deposition happened at the same time.

## 6. Linking climate and history

Coinciding changes can motivate a causal hypothesis. Examine food, water, land use, institutions and other mediating processes. Match region, season and temporal resolution. A cold period and a social change can coincide without establishing a cause. Investigate the processes that might connect them, including which season matters to crops or water supply. Check that the climatic and historical sources refer to comparable places, seasons and time resolution.

## 7. Case: wooden containers and dated wooden slips

Maeda and colleagues (2024) compare Nara wooden containers and wooden slips. Tree-ring dates are compared with written dates. Read how different sources constrain feature chronology. This study compares scientific dating and written evidence within archaeological contexts. Ask how independently the two types of evidence constrain the date and how their shared context is established. Follow the linked paper for its figures and detailed sample information.

## 8. Comparing outer-ring and written dates

What event does the written year describe?. What outer wood is preserved in the ring-dated object?. Examine association, duration of use and redeposition. A written year also requires interpretation: what event was being recorded? Likewise, the meaning of an outer-ring date depends on what wood survives. Compare the meanings of the dates, not just whether the numerals match, and assess how the objects came to be associated.

## 9. Reading Nabunken's public records

Connect images, measurement paths and numerical records. Check sample identifiers, context, species and preservation. Verify rather than assume correspondence with a paper's samples. Public data allow a reader to trace numerical measurements back to the inspected material. However, a similar site name does not establish that a database item and a paper use the same samples. Check identifiers and documentation before transferring interpretations between the two.

## 10. An ecological view of growth changes

Suppression may involve climate, insects or competition. Combine scars, recruitment, mortality and management records. Examine comparison trees and survivorship bias. Recall the Lesson 6 examples. Trees that survived and recovered may not represent the full impact on a forest. Dead trees, management history and other evidence help compare explanations. A common growth pattern is useful evidence, but it still needs ecological context.

## 11. The scope of a climate reconstruction

State region, season, climate variable and units. Check calibration, verification and response stability. Test the links from local climate to broader social change. Using a reconstruction in historical research does not remove the original model's limits. Read independent verification as well as calibration performance. Preserve the stated seasonal and spatial target when comparing the curve with documentary or archaeological evidence.

## 12. Exercise A: interpret three timber dates

All examples are fictional. A building record mentions 1210. Read the last preserved rings and bark status of A, B and C. 8 minutes: claims, limits and additional evidence. Suppose these timbers were recovered from different parts of the present building. Pause and consider how their dates relate to the written record. Avoid assigning exact felling years when bark is absent. Keep repair, reuse, source interpretation and possible dating errors among the explanations to check.

## 13. Exercise A: the limits of the conclusion

A: timber use after formation of the 1187 ring; felling unresolved. B: the 1199 ring formed; inspect season and interval before use. C: wood formed through 1213; reassess its relation to the 1210 record. If the 1213 dating is secure, that timber could not already have been used in the building in 1210. Later repair is one possible explanation, but the table does not establish it. Inspect structural position, joints, working traces, the documentary claim and dating quality.

## 14. Sources of uncertainty

Measurement and crossdating error. Missing information from preservation and sampling. Alternative environmental and historical interpretations. Uncertainty is not always captured by one numerical interval. A ring can be dated securely while the amount of lost outer wood or the storage interval remains unknown. State which parts of the reasoning are well constrained and which remain unresolved.

## 15. Diagramming a causal hypothesis

Show proposed processes connecting climate, trees, production and activity. Consider management, land use, institutions and other pathways. Arrows indicate hypotheses, not proven causal effects. A diagram helps expose missing steps. Production, exchange or institutions may mediate a relationship between climate and society. For each proposed arrow, ask what observation could support or weaken it. The diagram organizes a hypothesis; drawing it does not verify it.

## 16. Making a question specific

Specify place, period and material. State competing hypotheses and predicted observations. Identify evidence that could change the conclusion. A broad question such as whether climate affected history needs to become a specific comparison. Define a place, period, phenomenon and source. Include the observations expected under alternative explanations and decide how contradictory evidence would lead you to revise the claim.

## 17. Planning sources and sampling

Check source location, preservation and access conditions. Plan trees, sites, comparisons and sampling positions. Prepare alternatives if the preferred material is unavailable. A promising question still depends on suitable surviving material. Public images or existing measurements may offer alternatives to new sampling. Check access and preservation needs for the actual objects involved, and include a workable alternative if the preferred material cannot be examined.

## 18. Matching questions to methods

Calendar dating needs crossdating and an anchored reference. Disturbance studies combine growth, injuries, populations and comparisons. Climate estimation needs instrumental calibration and independent testing. A list of method names is not yet a research plan. Explain which part of the question each method addresses and which other evidence it needs. Fine measurement resolution does not automatically provide equally fine resolution for an event or historical interpretation.

## 19. Tracing papers, reports and datasets

Move from the abstract to methods, materials, figures and limits. Record dataset version, identifiers and the subset used. Distinguish primary reports from introductory or secondary accounts. An introductory article is useful for discovering a study, while the original report provides its methods and conditions. Record the particular database entry and subset used, not just the portal address. Use the bilingual glossary to expand search terms and find primary material in both languages.

## 20. Presenting figures, data and sources

Make plots with clear axes, units and sample depth. Distinguish measurements, synthetic data and schematics. Check the applicable conditions and credits for external figures. Redrawing a graph does not remove the need to credit its source data. Make it clear which parts are observations and which are settings chosen to explain an idea. For an external photograph or figure, check the conditions applying to that particular item and retain the required credits.

## 21. Exercise B: build your research plan

Write the question, sources, methods, comparisons, limits and additional evidence. Choose one or more archaeological, ecological or climate perspectives. 8 minutes: draft 5, discuss 2, revise 1. Pause and choose a topic related to your own interests. You can begin before locating every source if you specify what evidence is needed. Avoid writing the desired conclusion first. Describe the results that would support the proposed explanation and those that would require revising it.

## 22. Reviewing research plans

Does the evidence address the question?. Are comparisons and independent checks included?. Are alternatives and unavailable-material contingencies considered?. Focus less on whether you agree with a proposed explanation and more on whether its testing procedure is clear. Ask for one observation that could change the conclusion. If working alone, return to the draft later and review it using the same questions.

## 23. Structuring the report

The syllabus indicates 2–4 A4 pages and at least three references. Connect question, sources, methods, discussion and limits. Distinguish a proposal and expectations from completed results. The report should let a reader follow the reasoning from the question to the conclusion. If measurements have not been made, present the materials and procedures as a proposal rather than as completed findings. The syllabus requirements guide the format; this slide does not introduce additional grading weights or deadlines.

## 24. Conclusions and next checks

State the conclusion supported by the available evidence. Identify remaining alternatives and uncertainty. Name one further observation that could change the conclusion. Stating limits clarifies what has been learned and what remains unresolved. A useful conclusion identifies the evidence it depends on and the alternatives it has not excluded. Naming one specific additional observation turns those limits into a practical next research step.

## 25. Keeping records for reanalysis

Preserve identifiers, original measurements, units and missing values. Track the relationship among processing, figures, text and versions. Clarify supplied materials and release scope in collaborative work. Keep the original numbers as well as final figures so the analysis can be revised. Consistent identifiers connect measurements to images and measurement paths. In collaborative work, record which materials were supplied by whom and the scope under which they can be shared or released.

## 26. Sources for continued learning

Use bilingual terms to search papers and data. Connect Nabunken records, tree-ring databases and primary papers. Continue testing explanations against observed material. The materials provide an entry to research literature and actual specimens. Bilingual search terms help connect local questions with studies elsewhere. After reading an explanation, return to the material and ask which visible or measurable feature would help test it.

## 27. Final reflection

How do you distinguish a ring date from an event date?. Can you compare two explanations for one observation?. What new evidence could change your research conclusion?. Return to the opening question about why past dates and environments matter. What longer perspective can tree rings bring to a present question? Answer these three prompts and choose the part of your research plan that most needs further evidence.

## 28. Tree rings, environment and historical questions

Record observations, verify dates and compare explanations. Move among specimens, data and literature. Present a question together with the limits of its answer. Across the eight lessons we have moved from reading rings to combining several kinds of environmental and historical evidence. Careful observations, secure dating and explicit alternatives are useful far beyond this course. Apply them when reading new sources and developing questions of your own.
