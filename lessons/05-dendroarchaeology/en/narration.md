## 1. Dendroarchaeology

Dendroarchaeology. This explanation supports a ninety-minute dendroarchaeology lesson. Pause to complete the dating and interpretation exercises.

## 2. Learning objectives

Explain the questions addressed by dendroarchaeology. Distinguish outer-ring, felling and construction dates. Use preservation and context to state conclusions and limits. The aim is to explain the evidence linking a numerical date to an event, not merely to report a year.

## 3. From measurement to archaeological interpretation

Crossdating assigns calendar years to preserved rings. The last measured ring may not be the original outermost ring. Now interpret the dates in their archaeological context. Even with secure measurement and crossdating, interpreting construction or repair requires the object's structural and archaeological context.

## 4. Ring years and event dates

Ring formation. Felling. Working / storage. Construction / use. Repair / reuse / deposition. Intervals can separate the stages shown. Before equating felling with use, ask what evidence constrains the time between them.

## 5. Inspect surviving outer wood

Bark edge survives: approach the final growth increment. Some sapwood survives: missing outer rings may be estimated. Heartwood only: a precise felling date is harder to establish. The meaning of the last preserved ring depends on whether bark, sapwood or only inner wood survives.

## 6. What does a surviving bark edge establish?

Inspect the outermost increment beneath the bark. Check incomplete growth and possible cutting after tree death. Relate final growth to felling only with supporting conditions. A complete outer edge tightly constrains felling, but intra-ring position and dormancy can still matter to the event interval.

## 7. Estimating missing sapwood

Identify the heartwood–sapwood boundary. Use a sapwood model appropriate to species and population. Do not transfer another region’s ring-count range uncritically. Estimating missing sapwood requires appropriate species and regional information, rather than adding a universal number of rings.

## 8. Interpreting a lower date bound

A terminus post quem is a lower bound, not an upper bound. A last surviving ring of 1580 excludes felling before 1580. Unknown missing rings leave the upper bound unresolved. A lower bound indicates that the wood formed through that year. The amount of missing outer growth remains a separate issue.

## 9. Considering the felling season

Examine earlywood and latewood development. Growth timing varies with species, site and climate. A winter interval may span two calendar years. Felling-season inference examines the developmental state of outer cells and requires evidence about preservation and the growth season.

## 10. Sampling within a building

Link member position, joints, tool marks and repair records. Seek preserved outer wood and multiple members. Consider whether different members came from the same tree. Position, joints, working traces and repair evidence help determine what a cluster of timber dates represents.

## 11. Exercise A: how tightly can you date it?

A: incomplete 1600 ring; complete bark edge. B: last ring 1580; heartwood only; outer loss unknown. C: heartwood ends in 1590; five sapwood rings; assume 15–25 in total. Pause and separate the securely constrained interval from the claims that would require additional assumptions for each sample.

## 12. Exercise A: intended interpretations

A: felling in the 1600 growth season, under the stated conditions. B: felling in or after 1580; no upper bound established. C: approximately 1605–1615, conditional on the supplied model. The same numerical date can have different implications when outer preservation differs. Compare the conditions behind each interpretation.

## 13. Members are not necessarily independent trees

Several boards or beams can come from one tree. Compare patterns, anatomy and member information. Distinguish possible same-tree matches from independent dating evidence. Several members may come from one tree, so their number does not equal the number of independent trees.

## 14. Compare date groups with building phases

Plot dates together with member locations. Compare them with proposed building phases. Ask whether younger timber marks repair, extension or replacement. A date cluster can suggest a building phase, but that interpretation needs support from structure and other records.

## 15. Interpreting older timber

Reused timber can predate its current structural use. Inspect redundant joints, tool marks, dimensions and location. An older date alone does not prove reuse. An older timber can reflect storage, reuse or surviving earlier fabric. Compare these explanations rather than selecting one immediately.

## 18. Timber provenance is a separate question

Dendroprovenancing compares candidate source regions. The best match need not identify a unique origin. Consider reference coverage, timber trade, species and anatomy. A regional chronology match does not by itself establish timber origin. Consider broad climate coherence and the available reference coverage.

## 19. Reading a Japanese report: Horyuji

Mitsutani (2001) reports a 594 CE date for the central pillar. Three further outer rings extend the measured sequence ending in 591. What evidence connects a member date to the date of the building?. Consult the primary report for material condition, the event constrained and the wording of its conclusion, rather than filling gaps from a summary.

## 20. Reading an English report: Rook Hall Farm

Complete sapwood in one sample supports winter 1522/23 felling. The report suggests construction in 1523 or within a year or two. Separate the felling result from the construction interpretation. Reports in another language can be read using the same checks: material, measurement, dating evidence and structural context.

## 21. Record undated material as well

Short series, weak patterns or missing references can prevent dating. Dated members may not represent the whole structure. State what remains undated and propose further observations. Unsuccessful dating has causes worth recording. Reporting only successful samples can give a misleading picture of the available material.

## 22. Evidence to include in a report

Specimen ID, location, species and outer-edge preservation. Reference, overlap, candidate dates, mismatches and checks. Type of conclusion, assumptions, alternatives and references. Explain the path from sample location through the outer-ring result to felling or construction inference so a reader can evaluate it.

## 23. Exit questions

Does heartwood ending in 1580 establish construction in 1580?. Where do you add a total sapwood estimate if five rings already survive?. Give a reason and one useful additional observation for each. Distinguish the outer-ring date, felling and construction, and identify the further evidence needed for each inference.
