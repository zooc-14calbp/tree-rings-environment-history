# Principles of dendrochronology

## 1. Principles of dendrochronology

Principles of dendrochronology. This video provides the explanation for a ninety-minute classroom lesson. Pause during the crossdating exercises and inspect the sequences yourself.

## 2. Learning objectives

Explain shared growth patterns. Relate limiting factors to replication. Crossdate rings. Match multiple features and detect a shift caused by an absent ring. Support a dating decision. Distinguish calendar anchoring, overlap length and independent checks. The main idea is to verify a sequence of matching features, rather than relying on one striking ring.

## 3. Review: boundaries and dates

Tree-ring boundary. Identify an annual increment from anatomical changes. False and locally absent rings. Counting one radius can introduce extra or missing years. Question for today. What can comparison with other trees establish?. A mistaken boundary can affect both width measurements and the calendar assignments of subsequent increments.

## 4. Counting and crossdating

Approach. What it establishes. Ring counting. Counts visible increments; missed or extra boundaries can shift the result. Crossdating. Tests ring dates by matching shared patterns among series. Calendar anchoring. Connects the sequence to a securely dated reference. Counting gives the number of visible increments. Crossdating tests their individual years using shared variation in other series.

## 5. Factors that limit growth

Water availability can limit growth at dry sites. Growing-season temperature can limit growth at cold sites. The relevant factor varies with species, site and period. Limiting factors differ among sites and species. Shared environmental responses provide one basis for matching growth patterns.

## 6. Choosing sites and trees for the question

Climate questions. Select sites where trees respond to the climate variable of interest. Forest-history questions. Look for trees and spatial patterns recording competition or disturbance. Conditions for comparison. The same species or region does not guarantee matching growth. Choose trees for the required period and environmental information, not simply because they are old.

## 7. Several influences contribute to ring width

Age and stem size. Shared environmental variation. Competition, disturbance and individual effects. Ring-width variation. Several influences contribute to width. Finding a shared pattern and identifying its cause are distinct tasks.

## 8. Uniformitarianism and testing growth responses

Uniformitarianism. Use present processes to help interpret past records. Responses may change. Age, site and climate changes can alter growth responses. Reconstruction needs further tests. Verify dating and environmental interpretation separately. Applying a modern relationship to the past requires checking its conditions. It is not an assumption that every response remains unchanged.

## 9. Replication across radii, trees and sites

Multiple radii of one tree. Different trees. Different sites. Four samples need not represent four trees. Several radii help inspect local features, while additional trees provide different replication. Keep those information sources distinct.

## 10. The basis of crossdating

Compare the sequence of variation in different trees. Absolute widths can differ while shared features remain. Compare rises and falls around narrow rings as well as the narrow rings themselves.

## 11. From one marker year to a sequence

A single narrow-ring match is insufficient. Check surrounding variation, spacing and the whole overlap. A narrow ring can recur many times. A matching sequence of several features provides stronger evidence for choosing among alignments.

## 12. Skeleton plots

Narrow rings receive longer marks relative to neighbours. Normal rings can remain unmarked; compare feature spacing. Marks represent relative features, not measured widths. A skeleton plot simplifies selected features for comparison. Keep a connection to the original specimen and observations.

## 13. Reference chronologies and calendar anchoring

Begin with securely dated material, such as living trees. Verify overlaps and extend the record with older wood. An unanchored sequence is a floating chronology. Connected sequences can remain floating unless linked to known calendar years. Inspect the evidence for anchoring the chronology.

## 14. Crossdating exercises

A: a complete sequence. Match 30 rings of A to reference M, 1950–1999. B: a sequence with a shift. Match 31 observed rings and locate the inconsistency. Teaching conditions. All data are synthetic. These short sequences practise a procedure, not real-world dating criteria. Pause and record both a candidate position and the sequence features supporting it.

## 15. Exercise A: locating 30 rings

Propose the first and last ring years. Identify at least three matching features and compare another placement. Maintain consistent annual spacing when aligning the series, and check whether a promising match creates contradictions elsewhere.

## 16. Exercise A: the intended match

A: 1962–1991, 30 rings. Several features align at matching intervals. If your answer differs, inspect the sequence and endpoint counting. Compare the reasoning, not only the selected start year.

## 17. A one-year shift within a sequence

The first part may match while the later part diverges. Investigate missed boundaries, locally absent rings or false rings. Do not insert or delete values merely to improve a match. If alignment works initially but shifts later, investigate boundary errors, measurements and locally absent increments.

## 18. Exercise B: locating the shift

Compare segments of the 31 observed rings. Can one constant shift align both early and late segments?. Record where to re-examine boundaries and possible causes. Pause to locate the shift. Before adding an increment, consider which specimen observations would verify that decision.

## 19. Exercise B: a missing 1974 increment

B spans 1958–1989: 31 observed rings across 32 calendar years. Reserve the 1974 position between 1973 and 1975. Keep it missing; do not invent an observed width of zero. The missing increment is deliberately specified in this synthetic example. Real decisions need support from other radii or independent trees.

## 20. Samples used to verify a match

Another radius of the same tree. Investigate local absences and boundary-reading errors. A different tree. Check whether the pattern is reproduced. A reference excluding the unknown. Do not use agreement with a reference already containing that sample as independent proof. Check independent material as well as agreement with the same reference chronology to strengthen the dating evidence.

## 21. Visual and statistical checks

Correlation helps compare candidate placements. Its interpretation depends on overlap, preprocessing and search range. One high coefficient proves neither the date nor its cause. Statistics help identify candidates, but short overlaps and few distinctive features can produce chance matches.

## 22. Overlap length and available evidence

Short overlaps provide fewer ways to reject chance matches. Required length depends on pattern, species, region and other evidence. A 30-ring exercise does not establish a 30-ring dating rule. The same correlation over different overlap lengths represents different evidence. Inspect duration and the distribution of distinctive features together.

## 23. When a sample does not match

Re-examine the evidence. Check surfaces, images, boundaries, measurements and transcription. Assess reference suitability. Check species, region, period and whether shared variation is expected. Retain an unresolved result. Do not force calendar dates when evidence is insufficient. Do not force an unmatched sample into a date. Review boundaries, species, region and reference applicability.

## 24. Records supporting a dating decision

Sample ID, sampling position and observed boundaries. Reference and version, candidate dates and overlap. Matching and mismatching features, with rechecks. Handling of ring anomalies, analyst and date. Retain candidates, mismatches and reinspection locations so another reader can evaluate the dating decision later.

## 25. Summary

Shared variation supports matching. Growth also contains individual and disturbance effects. Test ring dates with multiple features. Observed ring counts can differ from the calendar span. Replication and independent checks matter. Investigate mismatches as well as apparent agreement. Dating combines visual inspection, statistical support, replication and calendar anchoring, with none of those steps standing alone.
