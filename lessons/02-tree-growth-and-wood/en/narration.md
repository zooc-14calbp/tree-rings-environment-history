# Tree growth, wood anatomy and tree-ring structure

## 1. Tree growth, wood anatomy and tree-ring structure

Tree growth, wood anatomy and tree-ring structure. This explanation supports a ninety-minute classroom lesson. Distinguish organs, tissues and cells, and pause the video to complete the observation exercises.

## 2. Learning objectives

Locate wood production. Explain the vascular cambium and secondary growth in a diagram. Read boundaries from anatomy. Compare softwood, ring-porous and diffuse-porous wood using visible features. Interpret a ring count. Consider sampling height, missing pith, false rings and absent rings. Use visible features to explain the material rather than only memorizing terms. Record observations separately from the interpretations you propose.

## 3. Review: what event does a ring date?

Year of ring formation. Link this to felling or construction using additional evidence. Annual increments. Check whether a growth ring represents one year. Question for today. Will simply counting dark lines give a correct date?. A calendar date for a ring does not necessarily date germination or felling with the same precision. Consider what part of the tree survives.

## 4. A tree is a connected living organism

Leaves: photosynthesis and gas exchange. Stem: support, transport and storage. Roots: water and nutrient uptake. Photosynthesis, water uptake and stem transport are connected. Interpret stem growth as part of a living organism rather than an isolated clock.

## 5. Growth in length and thickness

Primary growth Apical meristems extend shoots and roots. Secondary growth Cambial activity adds wood and thickness. Growth in length and growth in thickness involve different meristems and directions. Keep their positions distinct when reading the diagram.

## 6. The cambium produces wood

Inward: secondary xylem. Outward: secondary phloem. The cambium lies between xylem and phloem. The cambium produces xylem inward and phloem outward. Locate these tissues in the schematic before interpreting boundaries in real material.

## 7. Wood also contains living cells

Tissue or cell. Typical state and function at maturity. Tracheids / vessel elements. Usually dead; involved in water conduction and support. Sapwood parenchyma. Includes living cells for storage and metabolism. Cambial cells. Living meristem supplying xylem and phloem cells. Wood contains cell types with different functions and lifetimes. Distinguish mature conducting cells from living parenchyma rather than treating all wood alike.

## 8. Sapwood and heartwood

Sapwood Outer wood involved in water transport and storage. Heartwood Inner, transformed wood, usually no longer conducting water. A colour change is not itself a growth-ring boundary. The sapwood-heartwood boundary is different from an annual growth boundary. Colour alone does not establish the year a tree was felled.

## 9. Ring counts depend on sampling height

Upper stem sections form later. A higher sample can contain a shorter record. Distinguish a ring count from age since germination. A tree takes time to reach the sampling height. The ring count at that height alone does not measure the full time since germination.

## 10. Seasonality and wood formation

Dormancy and reactivation. Cell production and maturation. Activity declines toward dormancy. Seasonal changes in cell dimensions and wall thickness help make growth increments recognizable. Interpret boundaries through those anatomical changes.

## 11. From cell production to mature wood

Cell division. Cell enlargement. Secondary wall formation and lignification. Maturation. A newly divided cell is not finished wood. Enlargement and wall formation occur in stages that overlap through the growing season.

## 12. The three directions: L, R and T

L: longitudinal. R: radial. T: tangential. Distinguishing longitudinal, radial and tangential directions helps identify the orientation of a section or image within the original tree.

## 13. Three planes reveal the same tissue

Transverse section R–T plane, perpendicular to L. Radial section L–R plane. Tangential section L–T plane. The same tissue looks different in different cutting planes. Relate the section name to the three-dimensional arrangement of its cells.

## 14. The shape of water-conducting cells

Tracheid An elongated cell linked to neighbours through pits. Vessel A chain of vessel elements connected through perforations. Vessel elements and tracheids differ in shape and arrangement. Examine their context as well as the size of an individual opening.

## 15. Softwood: examine tracheids

Examples: sugi and hinoki. Earlywood tends to have larger lumens and thinner walls. Latewood tends to have smaller lumens and thicker walls. In the softwood schematic, follow changes in tracheid lumen size and walls. Real specimens also require attention to species and sampling position.

## 16. Ring-porous wood: locate earlywood vessels

Examples: chestnut and konara oak. Large vessels are concentrated near the start of an increment. Examine the distribution, not just a single row of pores. Large earlywood vessels are useful clues in ring-porous wood. Confirm them against the anatomical sequence across the whole growth increment.

## 17. Diffuse-porous wood: look beyond vessels

Examples: beech and maples. Vessel sizes are comparatively similar within an increment. Fibre and parenchyma patterns can help locate boundaries. Vessel size alone may not reveal a boundary in diffuse-porous wood. Inspect arrangement and changes in other tissues as well.

## 18. Compare three wood types

Softwood. Ring-porous. Diffuse-porous. The three wood types differ in the anatomical evidence used to locate boundaries, not simply in how easy or difficult their rings appear.

## 19. A ring is an increment of wood

A yearly increment includes earlywood and latewood. A tree-ring boundary separates adjacent annual increments. A dark band alone is not the whole yearly increment. Counting thin lines alone can miss important structure. Think of a ring as a band of wood with width and internal anatomical variation.

## 20. Where should the boundary be placed?

Latewood of the older increment → earlywood of the next. Follow changes in lumen size and wall thickness. Check anatomy and continuity when colour is ambiguous. Use a consistent boundary convention and explain the anatomical feature supporting it. Check whether another measurement path gives a different view.

## 21. False rings: a boundary-like feature within a ring

False ring. Cell anatomy may change and then return within one growing season. Check other radii and trees, and verify through crossdating. A boundary-like change within an increment need not indicate another year. Compare other radii and trees before counting it as annual.

## 22. Locally absent rings: an increment pinches out

Locally absent ring. Visible ring counts can differ between radii of one tree. A single radius can miss an increment. An increment can become locally absent around a stem. Its absence along one radius does not mean that the calendar year is missing.

## 23. Observation exercise

Record observations first. Mark pore size, distribution, wall thickness and changes. Then interpret. Propose a wood type and boundaries with supporting evidence. Keep uncertainty visible. State which plane, magnification or sample would help. Pause and describe or sketch the feature used to identify a boundary. The exercise is about evidence rather than merely naming a wood type.

## 24. Observation A: three schematic samples

Which wood type is represented by A, B and C?. Mark boundary candidates and give your evidence. These are schematic examples. Record visible openings, wall changes and arrangements before deciding which interpretation best fits the material.

## 25. Observation B: which boundary separates years?

Compare candidates 1, 2 and 3. What additional evidence would resolve the interpretation?. Pause and justify the boundaries you select. Avoid treating every similar-looking line as another year without checking its anatomical context.

## 26. Discuss observations and interpretations

Sample. Interpretation. Evidence and further checks. A. Ring-porous. Large vessels recur near the start of increments. B. Softwood. An observation describes what is visible; an interpretation proposes what it means. Make the distinction clear enough for another observer to check.

## 27. What can 42 rings tell us?

At 1.3 m: bark present, pith missing. 42 visible rings. This does not establish 42 years since germination. Assess missing inner wood, years to reach height, annuality and absent rings. Sampling height and missing pith affect the relation between ring count and age. State those conditions when interpreting a count as age evidence.

## 28. Summary

The cambium adds xylem inward. Wood contains cells involved in transport, support and storage. Read boundaries from anatomy. Use criteria appropriate to the wood type and plane. A count alone does not fix a date. Consider sample position, missing wood and ring anomalies. Connecting cambial position, wood anatomy and boundary decisions allows you to explain what a measured ring width actually represents.
