## 4. Start sampling with the question

Climate. Choose species, sites and comparison areas relevant to the climate variable. Forest history. Plan how spatial sampling will capture disturbance and regeneration. Buildings and objects. Record member location, working or reuse, and surviving inner and outer wood. The event of interest determines which positions, preserved edges and comparison material are needed.

## 5. What material will be available?

Increment core. One radial record; document sampling position, losses and injuries. Disc or block. Allows comparison across radii and scars; define the sampled extent. Surface image. Records the readable surface; requires a scale and imaging conditions. Cross-sections and cores preserve different views. Plan whether the available material can answer the question before collecting it.

## 6. What a core does and does not record

Did the core reach the pith?. Is the outermost ring preserved?. Could bending, breakage or compression affect the record?. A feature absent on one core may survive elsewhere around the tree. Record position and orientation.

## 7. Link measurements to specimen metadata

Identification. Link site, tree, specimen and path IDs to images and containers. Collection and condition. Date, species, member position, height, direction, pith, bark and losses. Processing and measurement. Preparation, image calibration, operator, boundaries, revisions and reasons. Use a consistent identifier to connect photographs, collection records and measurements, while retaining the contextual information behind the number.

## 9. Preserve order during transport and storage

Identify immediately. Link specimen and container IDs; retain orientation and fragment order. Handle for the condition. Record cracks, decay and moisture; minimise distortion and mixing. Record the initial state. Save photographs and collection records, including missing material. Losing order or bark-side orientation can make later interpretation much harder, even when the wood itself survives.

## 10. Prepare a surface with readable boundaries

Mount and orient. Support the core after checking its orientation and cellular structure. Prepare the surface. Use preparation suited to the material to reveal boundaries and cells. Inspect the result. Distinguish scratches and preparation marks from narrow increments. Prepare a readable surface without losing the features needed for interpretation, and document how preparation altered the specimen.

## 11. The path relative to ring boundaries

An oblique path gives a longer distance between boundaries. Adjust the path to the local orientation of the boundaries. Preserve the measured path in images and records. An oblique path changes the measured distance. Explain how the path relates to the intended direction of ring width.

## 12. Convert pixels to a physical length

A known 10 mm spans 600 pixels in the image. Spatial scale: 600 divided by 10 = 60 px/mm. Width (mm) = boundary separation in pixels divided by 60. Check that the scale applies to the image and transformation. Converting pixels into length requires calibration under the relevant imaging conditions. Check the resulting units.

## 13. From boundary positions to ring widths

Seven boundary positions define six widths. Subtract successive positions, then apply the spatial scale. Positions increase from inner to outer wood in this example. A width is the difference between consecutive boundary positions. Do not confuse the positions themselves with their differences.

## 16. Quality control links numbers and observations

Units, precision and order. Check pixels versus mm, orientation, duplicate or missing rows and delimiters. Suspicious values. Investigate extremes, jumps and repeated values in the original image. Reasons for revision. Recheck boundaries or transcription; preserve the change and its reason. An unusual value calls for checking the original image, boundaries, injuries and units, rather than immediate deletion.

## 17. Connect measurement with dating checks

Assign candidate calendar years. Match a dated reference and record overlap and mismatches. Return to the specimen. Investigate local absences, false rings, missed boundaries or measurement shifts. Separate observations and interpretation. Keep missing-position and dating hypotheses separate from observations. Measurement revisions can affect crossdating. Keep a record of which values changed and why.

## 18. Growth levels differ among trees

Width reflects species, size, individual history and other influences. A raw mean alone need not isolate the signal of interest. Different widths in the same year do not automatically indicate different dates. Distinguish growth level from the sequence of variation.

## 19. Standardization relative to a growth curve

Here, index = ring width divided by supplied growth-curve value. An index of 1 matches the curve; 1.2 is 1.2 times it. The curve choice affects the variation retained. Dividing by a reference growth curve produces a dimensionless index. The chosen reference affects which variations remain.

## 20. Which variation is retained?

A is indexed against two different reference curves. A flexible reference can remove longer-term variation. Record the question, method and settings; compare results. A smooth fitted curve can remove slow variation of interest as well as an age trend.

## 21. Radii and the weighting of trees

Tree A has two radii: 0.8 and 1.2. Tree B has one: 1.5. Average A first: 1.0. The equally weighted tree mean is 1.25. A direct mean of all three radii is about 1.17, giving A more weight. Distinguish averaging within a tree from averaging among trees so that extra cores do not unintentionally give one tree excessive weight.

## 22. Show sample depth through time

Average the available tree indices in each calendar year. A continuous chronology can have changing sample depth. Tree count alone is insufficient; consider agreement and sampling bias. Earlier chronology sections may have fewer trees. Read the mean together with the number of independent trees supporting each year.

## 24. What can the chronology establish?

An index is not temperature or precipitation. Test environmental responses against independent evidence. A low index can have several causes. Distinguish drought, cold, disturbance and measurement problems. Review processing for the question. Removed variation may matter when studying growth release. A chronology documents shared growth variation, while its climatic or ecological cause requires additional evidence.

## 25. Keep results traceable to the observations

Specimen ID, images, boundaries, units and calibration. Dating evidence and revision history. Detrending settings and sample depth. Preserve observations and store derived results separately. Link original values, processed data and figures so the effect of alternative processing can be evaluated.
