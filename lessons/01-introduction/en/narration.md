## 3. Questions linking history and environment

When did it happen?. Place building repairs, forest changes and climate variation in time. Which came first?. Order and time lag affect the explanations we can test. What can the evidence show?. Distinguish simultaneous events from a causal explanation. To relate environmental change to human activity, we need to know not just what changed but also which event came first.

## 4. Date ranges and the order of events

Overlapping date ranges can leave the order unresolved. When date ranges overlap, the order of events may remain unresolved. Inspect the full ranges rather than only their central values.

## 5. Relations revealed by tighter dates

Tighter dates help us examine order, time lag and duration. Tighter dates reduce the possible sequences. Temporal proximity is useful evidence, but it does not establish a cause by itself.

## 6. Why study the past?

Understanding history. Compare documents, objects and structural timbers. Putting the present in context. Compare current conditions with variability beyond instrumental records. Informing decisions. Use histories of change to inform forest management and preparedness. Past records extend the perspective beyond short instrumental observations and allow comparisons with conditions different from those of today.

## 7. Relative and numerical ages

Relative age. An older-or-younger relationship. Example: lower and upper strata. Check for overturning and redeposition. Numerical age. An age expressed in years. Example: a ring formed in 1300 CE. Numerical dates retain assumptions and uncertainty. Knowing an order differs from assigning a numerical age or calendar date. Numerical results still have uncertainty that must be reported.

## 8. Sample age and event age

The year a ring formed can differ from the year a building was constructed. Trace the process connecting the dated material to the event of interest. The sample and the event need not have the same age.

## 9. Dating methods and their targets

Method. Sample or record. Dating evidence. Dendrochronology. Wood with growth rings. Matching ring patterns. Radiocarbon. Wood, seeds, bone. Choose a method by considering the surviving material and the event that started its clock, rather than selecting a technique by name alone.

## 10. Radioactive decay and half-life

After each half-life, half of the parent isotope remains. Each half-life halves the amount remaining again. This is an exponential process, unlike a linear decline that removes a fixed amount each time.

## 11. Radiocarbon dating

Sample. Examine carbon in suitable biological material, such as wood or seeds. Measurement. Use radiocarbon decay, while considering contamination and carbon sources. Interpretation. Calibrate the radiocarbon age and interpret a calendar-age range. A radiocarbon age is not a calendar date read directly from the material. Calibration and the sample's history both matter to its interpretation.

## 12. Dating rocks and carbonates

K–Ar / Ar–Ar. Suitable minerals in volcanic rocks. Consider clock closure and retention during cooling. Dating ash requires suitable minerals and depositional context. U–Th. Suitable carbonates, including speleothems. Use isotope changes after formation. Assess initial components and later exchange. For geological materials, identify the mineral and the event being dated. Different measurements can constrain different stages of a material's history.

## 13. Dating with light, heat and magnetism

Luminescence dating. Measure stored signals in minerals. OSL uses light resetting; TL commonly uses heating. Assess resetting and the burial dose-rate environment. Archaeomagnetic dating. Examine remanent magnetism in fired materials. Compare cooling-related magnetism with a regional reference. Check movement after firing and the site location. Methods involving light, heat or magnetism record different processes. Check which event is constrained and whether the material is suitable.

## 14. The role of dendrochronology

Match patterns of wide and narrow rings to assign calendar years. Dendrochronology matches sequences of variation and tests their connection to known calendar years. It involves more than counting visible rings.

## 15. Archives of past environments

Tree rings. Width, density, isotopes. Conditions experienced by trees. Ice cores. Isotopes, dust, gas bubbles. Climate and atmospheric records. Lake sediments. Pollen, chemistry, varves. Different archives record environmental conditions through different processes. Compare what each measures and the time interval represented by an observation.

## 16. Interpreting proxy evidence

Compare with observations. Establish which conditions the proxy responds to. Consider multiple influences. Ring width can reflect age and competition as well as climate. Check the range of application. Relationships can vary with region, season, species and timescale. Separate the measured quantity from the environmental interpretation and test their relationship using observations or other independent evidence.

## 17. Temporal resolution and age uncertainty

A record may resolve individual years while its calendar position remains uncertain. Closely spaced measurements do not guarantee equally precise ages. Read temporal resolution and dating uncertainty as separate properties of a record.

## 18. Comparing independent records

Align the timescales. Check years, seasons, calibration and reference years. Compare the same variable. Early-spring temperature differs from summer moisture conditions. Investigate mismatches. Consider uncertainty, response lag, regional variation and preservation. Check whether records represent the same area and season. Apparent disagreements can arise because they describe different environmental targets.

## 21. Reasons for choosing a method

Match the sample and clock. Which evidence fits: carbon, rings, magnetism or mineral signals?. Identify the dated event. Does the date concern formation, cooling, burial, felling or use?. Specify further checks. Consider contamination, missing outer rings, movement, resetting and context. Give more than the method's name. Explain why the material is suitable and state what the resulting date would leave unresolved.

## 22. Interpreting the exercise

A. Radiocarbon. Dates seed carbon. Assess its association with hearth use. B. Dendrochronology. Dates surviving rings. Assess missing outer wood and reuse. C. Archaeomagnetism, for example. Several methods may be applicable to a sample but date different processes. Compare them as distinct pieces of evidence rather than interchangeable numbers.

## 23. BP and calendar-age notation

1000 ± 30 BP. Example conventional radiocarbon age, referenced to 1950. Do not convert it to a calendar year by subtraction alone. cal BP / cal BCE / cal CE. Calibrated calendar ages. Record the curve and assumptions. cal BP also refers to years before 1950. 1300 CE. Example of a ring’s calendar year. Without a reference convention and units, the same number can mean different things. Specify whether a result is a measured age or a calendar date.

## 24. Key points

Choose methods for the sample and question. Different samples from one site can date different events. Separate dating from environmental interpretation. Establish when a record formed, then investigate what it records. State the conditions of an interpretation. Combine dates, context and independent evidence. Keep track of the evidence supporting the date, environmental interpretation and causal explanation. These are related but separate steps in the argument.
